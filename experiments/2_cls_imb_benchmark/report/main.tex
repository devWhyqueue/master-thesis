\begin{abstract}
\noindent Class imbalance in computational pathology depends on the input regime: labeled image patches support ordinary classification, whereas whole-slide image (WSI) diagnosis is commonly modeled as weakly supervised learning over patch-feature bags. This report benchmarks representative class-imbalance mitigation methods on four histopathology datasets---CAMELYON16, TCGA-UT, BRACS, and PANDA---presented in order of the diagnostic question they pose (detection, typing, subtyping, grading) and chosen to span the difficulty--imbalance plane while varying organ, label type, and annotation form. Imbalance is characterised throughout by the entropy-based measure $\Hnorm\in[0,1]$, reported separately at patch and slide level. All benchmarks fix a frozen Virchow2 encoder, a bounded per-slide evidence budget, and patient-disjoint splits, so that comparisons within each regime isolate the imbalance mechanism. The four datasets place the same methods at very different points of the plane. On CAMELYON16 (severe patch imbalance $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.83}$, near-balanced slide labels $\approx\num{0.03}$, separable detection task) every method reaches near-ceiling discrimination and the unweighted baseline is competitive. On TCGA-UT (mild imbalance $\approx\num{0.09}$, 32-class typing) centre-focused affinity loss (CFAL) reaches the highest patch-level macro F1, OKO set learning the highest balanced accuracy and the best raw NLL and Brier among patch methods, while RankMix and MDE-MIL lead the WSI-bag regime. On BRACS (mild patch imbalance $\approx\num{0.07}$, hard fine-grained subtyping) only OKO and CFAL separate from the CE baseline. On PANDA (mild imbalance, two targets of very different difficulty on the same biopsies) the no-mitigation baseline is competitive in both regimes. Together the four datasets show that the headroom for imbalance mitigation is governed by class separability in the frozen representation, not by the imbalance of the class distribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Class imbalance changes what a pathology classifier is rewarded for learning. When common tumor types dominate training data, high aggregate accuracy can coexist with unreliable rare-class recall, especially if evaluation emphasizes prevalence-sensitive metrics. Long-tailed learning and medical-image evaluation literature therefore motivate classwise recall, macro F1, balanced accuracy, and calibration-aware probability metrics as primary outcomes in imbalanced settings \citep{saini2023tacklingClass,zhang2023deepLong,mosquera2024classImbalance}.

Imbalance methods often assume a specific prediction unit. Image-level methods use labeled images, image embeddings, or directly generated image samples, whereas WSI-level methods use weak labels over bags of instances together with attention, instance ranking, or bag-level representation learning. The computational-pathology pipeline these regimes sit in---whole-slide imaging, tiling into patches, and weakly supervised multiple-instance learning over frozen foundation-model features---and the full method definitions, imbalance mechanisms, and dataset provenance are documented in two companion reports \citep{queisler2026datasets,queisler2026imbalanceMethods}; this report contributes the experimental results and cites them only where a definition is needed.

The benchmark asks one question across four datasets, presented in order of the diagnostic question they pose---CAMELYON16 detection, TCGA-UT typing, BRACS subtyping, PANDA grading: when the encoder is a strong frozen foundation model, which imbalance mechanism helps in each input regime, and what property of the data governs whether any mitigation helps at all? The four datasets span the difficulty--imbalance plane by design. CAMELYON16 is severely imbalanced at patch level but separable; TCGA-UT is a mildly imbalanced many-class typing anchor; BRACS is mildly imbalanced but hard; and PANDA supplies two mildly imbalanced targets of different difficulty on the same biopsies. This spread separates the roles of imbalance severity and class separability: if the imbalance of the class distribution governed mitigation headroom, CAMELYON16 should show the largest gains; if class separability governs it, CAMELYON16 should show the smallest gains despite being the most imbalanced.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}
% ============================================================
The four datasets are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}; this section states only what the benchmark consumes from each. Imbalance is quantified by the entropy-based measure $\Hnorm\in[0,1]$ (\num{0} = perfectly balanced, values closer to \num{1} = more skewed), reported separately at patch and slide level because the two can differ sharply when patch labels are derived from masks rather than inherited from the slide; its definition and the full native class distributions are given in \citet{queisler2026datasets}. All benchmarks use frozen Virchow2 \num{2560}-dimensional features (concatenation of CLS token and mean patch token, cast to float32) and patient-disjoint train/validation/test splits across three random seeds \citep{vorontsov2024virchow,zimmermann2024virchow2}.

\paragraph{CAMELYON16.}
Binary labels are mask-derived: each tile is labelled tumor or normal by intersecting it with the published annotation mask. Of \num{399} slides, \num{398} have usable tile evidence; the \num{20} tumor slides whose annotations are not exhaustive are excluded from the patch regime but retained in the WSI-bag regime, where the reliable slide-level label is used. The per-slide evidence budget is the empirical median of available tissue tiles (\num{7874})---two orders of magnitude larger than TCGA-UT's 30-tile cap, reflecting that a whole lymph-node section yields far more tissue than a single lesion ROI or tumor-region crop. Each slide is its own patient, so slide-disjoint and patient-disjoint splits coincide. The severe patch imbalance ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$) is a within-slide effect: tumor tiles are scarce even on slides that do contain a metastasis, so the slide-level labels are nearly balanced ($\approx\num{0.028}$).

\paragraph{TCGA-UT.}
The dataset contains \num{1608060} patches from \num{8736} diagnostic slides across 32 cancer types \citep{tomczak2015reviewThe,komura2022universalEncoding}. Splits are assigned at the TCGA participant level (case-disjoint), using \num{5025} training, \num{1075} validation, and \num{1075} test cases per seed; all slides from one participant remain in one partition. Because every patch inherits its slide's cancer-type label, the patch and slide imbalance coincide ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.085}$). The patch benchmark caps each slide at 30 deterministic patches (empirical median of available tiles at resolution level~0), yielding a controlled manifest shared by all patch methods; the WSI-bag benchmark applies the same 30-instance cap to each slide's feature bag.

\paragraph{BRACS.}
Evidence is derived from the ROI set: each annotated ROI is tiled into $256\times256$ RGB patches, and each slide's pooled ROI tiles are capped at the empirical median (\num{360} tiles per slide). This design preserves label fidelity because all instances originate from pathologist-selected lesion regions. Of \num{547} published WSIs, \num{387} contribute at least one ROI tile; the benchmark covers \num{151} patients and \num{99352} extracted patches over the seven-subtype target, a mild imbalance ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.072}$ patch, $\approx\num{0.018}$ slide).

\paragraph{PANDA.}
The WSI-bag regime uses the canonical six-class ISUP grade (0~=~benign, 1--5~=~increasing cancer grade groups; $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.043}$). The patch regime uses a provider-consistent binary cancer/benign target derived from pixel masks ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.332}$); because Radboud and Karolinska masks use institution-dependent value semantics, collapsing both to cancer/benign is the only label the two providers share. To keep frozen-feature extraction tractable, the benchmark uses a stratified subset of approximately \num{2000} biopsies, sampled to preserve the joint distribution of ISUP grade and provider. The per-slide evidence budget is the empirical median number of tissue tiles per biopsy (\num{421}); biopsies without available masks are excluded from the patch regime but kept in the WSI-bag regime.

\paragraph{Native imbalance is mild at the level the benchmark evaluates.}
Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview} makes a second point beyond the per-dataset summaries above: at
\emph{slide} level---the unit every diagnostic target in this benchmark is ultimately scored
on---all four datasets are only mildly skewed ($\Hnorm\in[0.018,0.085]$). The two large values in
the table (CAMELYON16 patch $0.832$, PANDA patch $0.332$) are within-slide, binary mask effects, not
properties of the typing, subtyping, or grading targets themselves. Figure~\ref{fig:native-imbalance-regimes}
places these eight numbers against the imbalance factors long-tailed CV benchmarks use to stress-test
the very mitigation methods evaluated here: Cui et al.\ construct long-tailed CIFAR-10/100 by an
exponential class-count decay at imbalance factor $\rho=N_{\max}/N_{\min}\in\{10,\dots,200\}$
\citep{cui2019classBalanced}, and Buda et al.\ study step and linear imbalance at comparable ratios
\citep{buda2018systematic}; at $\rho=100$ this places $\Hnorm$ at $0.29$ for CIFAR-10-LT and $0.13$
for CIFAR-100-LT (shaded band). Every native slide-level target sits below this band, and three of
the four sit below even the more conservative CIFAR-100-LT end. Benchmarking imbalance-mitigation
methods on these native distributions therefore under-tests them: the methods were designed for,
and validated on, a regime of imbalance the native slide-level targets do not reach. \S\ref{sec:controlled}
proposes a controlled-imbalance construction that closes this gap for a future benchmark iteration.

\medskip\noindent
All four datasets use three random seeds, deterministic per-slide evidence caps, and frozen Virchow2 features. Hyperparameters are selected independently per method: for each candidate configuration the validation macro F1 is averaged over the three seeds and the configuration with the highest mean is chosen (balanced accuracy as tie-breaker); a single configuration per method is then fixed before evaluating on the held-out test split. Results are reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across seeds, and patch and WSI-bag methods are compared only within their own regime.

The patch and WSI-bag classifier heads (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-models}) are shared across all datasets. The patch head is a two-layer MLP (linear map to 512 units, ReLU, dropout \num{0.1}, linear output to $C$ classes); CFAL keeps the trunk but replaces the final linear map with one learnable prototype per class and Gaussian affinity-based prediction. The WSI-bag head is an attention-based MIL network: each instance passes through a shared encoder ($2560\to 256$), instance weights come from a linear scorer with softmax over instances, the bag representation is their weighted sum, and slide logits come from a final linear map. SC-MIL adds a two-layer projection head of width 256; MDE-MIL keeps the attention pooling but uses two expert heads. The WSI-bag input is capped at the dataset-specific evidence budget (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}).

Macro F1 is the primary ranking metric within each regime because it weights classes equally and penalizes failure on rare classes; balanced accuracy and accuracy are reported alongside it. Three complementary probability-quality metrics---negative log likelihood (NLL), multiclass Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE)---are reported on held-out softmax outputs, both before and after a unified post-hoc temperature-scaling protocol in which, for each method and seed, a single temperature is fit on validation by minimizing NLL and applied unchanged to the test scores. Full metric definitions and the temperature-scaling protocol are given in \citet{queisler2026imbalanceMethods}.

\subsection{Controlled imbalance construction}
\label{sec:controlled}
Section~\ref{sec:data} showed that every slide-level target is only mildly imbalanced relative to
the CIFAR-LT regime the evaluated mitigation methods were designed for. Rather than accepting that
mismatch, each dataset can be restructured to a controlled, artificially skewed distribution using
two constructions from the long-tailed-recognition literature, both of which enforce a hard minimum
support $N_{\text{floor}}$ so that no class is starved below what is needed for stable evaluation.

\paragraph{Truncated exponential decay (Cui et al.).}
Ranking classes by native support, the target count for the $i$-th ranked class ($i=0,\dots,K-1$) is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:exp-decay}
  n_i \;=\; \max\bigl(N_{\text{floor}},\ \lfloor N_{\max}\,\mu^{\,i}\rfloor\bigr),
  \qquad \mu\in(0,1),
\end{equation}
with $\mu$ chosen to hit a target imbalance factor $\rho=N_{\max}/N_{\min}$ \citep{cui2019classBalanced}.
Unlike the original CIFAR-LT construction, which lets the tail decay to as few as a handful of images,
$N_{\text{floor}}$ caps the decay so every class retains enough slides for macro-averaged evaluation.

\paragraph{Step imbalance (Buda et al.).}
Classes are split into a head group, which keeps its native support, and a tail group, which is
uniformly downsampled to $N_{\text{floor}}$ \citep{buda2018systematic}. This isolates rarity as a
binary property (in the head group or not) rather than a continuous rank effect, and lets the tail
group be chosen for clinical relevance rather than by native rank.

\paragraph{Per-dataset strategy.}
Table~\ref{tab:controlled-targets} applies these constructions, plus simple ratio downsampling for
the binary CAMELYON16 case, to each dataset's native slide counts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TCGA-UT} (32 classes) uses exponential decay ranked by native support, floored at
    $N_{\text{floor}}=20$, so rare types such as DLBC and cholangiocarcinoma remain evaluable while
    the head-to-tail ratio widens well past its native $28.3$:1.
  \item \textbf{PANDA} (6 ordinal ISUP grades) uses the same decay but fixed to the ordinal grade
    order rather than re-ranked by support, so the artificial skew still respects grade order; the
    aggressive ISUP~4--5 grades are floored at $N_{\text{floor}}=50$.
  \item \textbf{BRACS} (7 subtypes) uses step imbalance: the benign/usual head group
    $\{$N, PB, UDH, IC$\}$ keeps native support, while the clinically critical atypical/malignant
    tail $\{$FEA, ADH, DCIS$\}$ is floored at $N_{\text{floor}}=30$.
  \item \textbf{CAMELYON16} (binary) is downsampled to a fixed \num{10}:1 normal:tumor ratio, floored
    at $N_{\text{floor}}=20$ tumor slides, simulating a low-prevalence screening scenario.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:controlled-construction} shows the resulting native-versus-target support for all
four datasets; Table~\ref{tab:controlled-targets} reports the resulting head-to-tail ratio and
$\Hnorm$. This construction is proposed as the basis for a future benchmark iteration; the results
in \S\ref{sec:results} still use the native distributions.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The four datasets are reported in the order of Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}. Each dataset carries one results float stacking the patch-feature and WSI-bag regimes, followed by three short paragraphs on patch-level results, bag-level results, and calibration. Absolute scores and method orderings are never comparable across the two regimes. TCGA-UT receives the deepest analysis; its tuning-sensitivity, paired-seed, and classwise-difficulty diagnostics, and all raw calibration tables, are collected in Appendices~\ref{app:additional-results} and~\ref{app:calibration}.

% -------- CAMELYON16 --------
\subsection{CAMELYON16 --- metastasis detection}

Table~\ref{tab:headroom} and Figure~\ref{fig:mitigation-headroom} collapse the four datasets to the comparison the report is built on, and three findings run across them.

\emph{Imbalance severity and mitigation headroom are decoupled---indeed inversely related here.} CAMELYON16 is by far the most imbalanced dataset at patch level ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$, an order of magnitude above the others) yet shows among the smallest mitigation deltas ($+\num{0.008}$ macro F1 at patch level), because its detection task is near-ceiling. The largest positive deltas appear instead where imbalance is \emph{mild} but the task is hard and classes are entangled: BRACS patch ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.072}$, $\Delta=+\num{0.039}$) and PANDA WSI-bag ISUP grading ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.043}$, $\Delta=+\num{0.025}$). Ranking these datasets by imbalance would predict the opposite of what is observed.

\emph{The patch/slide distinction is decisive, not incidental.} CAMELYON16's severe imbalance is entirely a within-slide, patch-level effect; at slide level its labels are nearly balanced ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.028}$) and the slide-level detection task is exactly where every method ceilings. A large patch-level imbalance therefore creates no headroom once the representation separates the two classes, which is why disambiguating patch- from slide-level imbalance is necessary before reading any single ratio as a difficulty signal.

\emph{The same axis governs which method family wins and how well-calibrated it is.} Where mild imbalance meets a hard, entangled patch task (TCGA-UT, BRACS), the methods that separate from the baseline are the ones that reshape the decision head or objective---CFAL prototypes and OKO set learning---while single-knob resampling, reweighting, and focal reweighting never separate from the baseline on any of the four datasets, and ProGAN augmentation lands within between-seed noise everywhere. Bag-aggregation methods (RankMix, MDE-MIL) help only where the bag task itself has headroom (TCGA-UT WSI). Calibration tracks the same separability axis: near-ceiling regimes reach post-scaling ECE near \num{0.001} (CAMELYON16 patch), while the hard grading and subtyping regimes retain residual post-scaling ECE up to \num{0.10}--\num{0.17}, so a single property---class separability in the frozen representation---governs discrimination headroom and probability quality together.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{(a) Frozen-encoder bottleneck.}
Once a strong frozen pathology encoder is fixed, patch-level gains come from reshaping the decision head or objective rather than from resampling or reweighting. CFAL replaces the linear head with learnable class prototypes and a margin objective, pulling examples toward compact class centres and penalising confusion with nearby wrong-class prototypes. OKO constructs training sets with one matched pair and $k$ distractors; averaging per-example logits across set members implicitly regularises logit magnitude and suppresses the over-confidence that afflicts CE-trained classifiers, which explains its combination of high balanced accuracy and the best raw NLL and Brier among patch methods. The simpler reweighting and resampling variants remain within the CE overlap band, consistent with the interpretation that exposure corrections address only part of the error pattern when the representation already separates most classes. ProGAN augmentation does not deliver a clear advantage despite pixel-level FID comparable to the source study \citep{ruizcasado2024enhancingHistopathological}; the decisive difference is that the source method trains its classifier end-to-end on pixels, whereas this benchmark feeds synthetic patches through a frozen encoder that may map slightly off-manifold synthetic patches into embedding regions the head rarely sees for real data. On WSI bags, RankMix and MDE-MIL improve on plain MIL CE in every seed, because the bottleneck there includes instance selection and bag aggregation, which mixing informative subsequences (RankMix) and coupling natural and balanced branches with cross-expert consistency (MDE-MIL) can address even when the underlying features are strong.

\paragraph{(b) Separability, not imbalance, is the bottleneck.}
The four datasets show that the imbalance of the class distribution does not predict whether mitigation helps. BRACS is mildly imbalanced ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.072}$ at patch level) but hard---fine-grained breast-lesion subtyping entangled in the frozen feature space---so only OKO and CFAL show a measurable patch-level lift. CAMELYON16 is severely imbalanced at patch level ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$) but easy---tumor and normal tiles are nearly separable in frozen Virchow2---so every method reaches near-ceiling discrimination and the unweighted baseline is competitive. PANDA provides the cleanest within-cohort control: two mildly imbalanced targets on the same biopsies diverge sharply in difficulty---binary detection (balanced accuracy $\approx\num{0.85}$) versus six-class ISUP grading (macro F1 $\approx\num{0.58}$)---while the no-mitigation baseline remains competitive in both. What governs the value of mitigation under a frozen-feature benchmark is thus class separability in the representation, not the imbalance of the distribution, and this is a direct caution against selecting or ranking imbalance-mitigation methods by imbalance ratio alone.

\paragraph{(c) ProGAN frozen-feature hypothesis.}
ProGAN augmentation is informative on BRACS and PANDA: the mild native imbalances never create the tail-scarcity regime where synthetic minority augmentation helped on TCGA-UT at harsher constructed severities. On BRACS (BAcc \num{0.460} versus CE \num{0.451}) and PANDA (macro F1 \num{0.855} versus CE \num{0.859}), ProGAN lands within between-seed noise of the CE baseline, neither helping nor hurting. This is consistent with the frozen-feature explanation rather than a generator-quality failure: the generators reach reasonable pixel-level FID, but their outputs must pass through a frozen encoder that may map slightly off-manifold synthetic patches into embedding regions the head rarely sees for real data.

\paragraph{(d) Limitations.}
TCGA-UT patch labels come from pathologist-selected tumor regions; they are useful patch-level class labels but not dense annotations. Within each dataset the two benchmarks differ in prediction unit and per-slide evidence budget, so their result tables are non-transferable. The validation sweep varies one imbalance-specific control per method and does not exhaust architecture or training-schedule choices, and both regimes use frozen features rather than end-to-end encoder adaptation; the benchmark is controlled rather than a full reproduction of each original paper. Calibration results for the BRACS and PANDA WSI-bag regimes are additionally affected by small test sets and fine-grained label sets rather than method-specific weaknesses.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================
This report benchmarked representative class-imbalance mitigation methods on four histopathology datasets spanning the difficulty--imbalance plane, using frozen Virchow2 features, bounded per-slide evidence budgets, and patient-disjoint splits throughout, and characterising imbalance by the entropy-based $\Hnorm$ at both patch and slide level. On CAMELYON16 every method reaches near-ceiling and the unweighted baseline is competitive despite the most severe patch imbalance in the suite; on TCGA-UT, CFAL reaches the highest patch-level macro F1 and OKO the highest balanced accuracy with the best raw NLL and Brier among patch methods, while RankMix and MDE-MIL lead the WSI-bag regime; on BRACS only OKO and CFAL separate from the CE baseline; and on PANDA both mildly imbalanced regimes are won by the no-mitigation baseline within between-seed noise. The practical implication is that the value of imbalance mitigation under a frozen-feature benchmark is governed by class separability in the representation, not by the imbalance of the class distribution. Imbalance mitigation should therefore be evaluated in the native input regime and alongside classwise recall and calibration, not only by aggregate discrimination, and method rankings should not be transferred across datasets on the basis of imbalance ratio alone.

    \emph{Patch} & $256\times256$ H\&E crop from a selected region; patch labels inherit the slide-level class.\\

    \emph{Case-disjoint split} & Split rule preventing any participant's slides, patches, or features from crossing train, validation, and test.\\
