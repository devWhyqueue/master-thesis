\begin{abstract}
\noindent Class imbalance in computational pathology depends on the input regime: labeled image patches support ordinary classification, whereas whole-slide image (WSI) diagnosis is commonly modeled as weakly supervised learning over patch-feature bags. This report benchmarks representative class-imbalance mitigation methods on four histopathology datasets---CAMELYON16, TCGA-UT, BRACS, and PANDA---presented in order of the diagnostic question they pose (detection, typing, subtyping, grading) and chosen to span the difficulty--imbalance plane while varying organ, label type, and annotation form. Imbalance is characterised primarily by the head-to-tail ratio $\rho=N_{\max}/N_{\min}$---the standard imbalance factor of the long-tailed-recognition literature---with the entropy-based $\Hnorm\in[0,1]$ as a class-count-invariant cross-check, both reported separately at patch and slide level. All benchmarks fix a frozen Virchow2 encoder, a bounded per-slide evidence budget, and patient-disjoint splits. On CAMELYON16 (the suite's highest native patch imbalance, $\rho\approx\num{39}$; near-balanced slide labels, $\rho\approx\num{1.5}$) every method reaches near-ceiling discrimination and the unweighted baseline is competitive. On TCGA-UT (mild imbalance, $\rho\approx\num{28}$, 32-class typing) centre-focused affinity loss (CFAL) reaches the highest patch-level macro F1, OKO set learning the highest balanced accuracy and the best raw NLL and Brier among patch methods, while RankMix and MDE-MIL lead the WSI-bag regime. On BRACS (mild patch imbalance, $\rho\approx\num{4.6}$, fine-grained subtyping) only OKO and CFAL separate from the CE baseline. On PANDA (mild imbalance, two targets of different difficulty on the same biopsies) the no-mitigation baseline is competitive in both regimes. Because these native comparisons confound task difficulty, training support, and imbalance, the controlled analysis fixes training-set size and evaluates imbalance-induced deficits against a balanced CE reference. Mitigation is then expressed as the fraction of that deficit recovered, with calibration and computational cost reported separately and independent feature-space probes used to test predictors of recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
Class imbalance changes what a pathology classifier is rewarded for learning. When common tumor types dominate training data, high aggregate accuracy can coexist with unreliable rare-class recall, especially if evaluation emphasizes prevalence-sensitive metrics. Long-tailed learning and medical-image evaluation literature therefore motivate classwise recall, macro F1, balanced accuracy, and calibration-aware probability metrics as primary outcomes in imbalanced settings \citep{saini2023tacklingClass,zhang2023deepLong,mosquera2024classImbalance}.

Imbalance methods often assume a specific prediction unit. Image-level methods use labeled images, image embeddings, or directly generated image samples, whereas WSI-level methods use weak labels over bags of instances together with attention, instance ranking, or bag-level representation learning. The computational-pathology pipeline these regimes sit in---whole-slide imaging, tiling into patches, and weakly supervised multiple-instance learning over frozen foundation-model features---and the full method definitions, imbalance mechanisms, and dataset provenance are documented in two companion reports \citep{queisler2026datasets,queisler2026imbalanceMethods}; this report contributes the experimental results and cites them only where a definition is needed.

This benchmark is organised around three research questions, evaluated across four datasets presented in order of the diagnostic question they pose---CAMELYON16 detection, TCGA-UT typing, BRACS subtyping, and PANDA grading. Each question concerns a controlled contrast in which the task, patient split, frozen encoder, training-set size, and held-out evaluation set are fixed:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 --- Effect of imbalance.] How does increasing training imbalance affect tail-class discrimination and calibration in patch classification and WSI-level MIL?
  \item[RQ2 --- Mitigation effectiveness.] Which methods recover the performance loss caused by training imbalance? What calibration and computational costs accompany that recovery?
  \item[RQ3 --- Predictors of mitigation headroom.] Do independently measured class separability and effective sample size predict mitigation recovery better than the head-to-tail ratio, and does this relationship differ between patch and WSI supervision?
\end{description}
RQ1 first tests whether imbalance creates a measurable deficit. RQ2 then conditions mitigation claims on that deficit rather than treating any improvement over imbalanced CE as recovered performance. RQ3 tests candidate explanations measured independently of the mitigation runs, avoiding the circular use of CE test performance as a proxy for separability. The four datasets span distinct combinations of label granularity, sample support, and apparent feature-space overlap; CAMELYON16 additionally permits a pathology-specific MIL analysis in which between-slide class imbalance and within-slide informative-instance sparsity are manipulated separately.

\paragraph{CAMELYON16.}
Binary labels are mask-derived: each tile is labelled tumor or normal by intersecting it with the published annotation mask. Of \num{399} slides, \num{398} have usable tile evidence; the \num{20} tumor slides whose annotations are not exhaustive are excluded from the patch regime but retained in the WSI-bag regime, where the reliable slide-level label is used. The per-slide evidence budget is the empirical median of available tissue tiles (\num{7874})---two orders of magnitude larger than TCGA-UT's 30-tile cap, reflecting that a whole lymph-node section yields far more tissue than a single lesion ROI or tumor-region crop. Each slide is its own patient, so slide-disjoint and patient-disjoint splits coincide. The severe patch imbalance ($\rho\approx\num{39}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$)---the highest in the suite---is a within-slide effect: tumor tiles are scarce even on slides that do contain a metastasis, so the slide-level labels are nearly balanced ($\rho\approx\num{1.5}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.028}$).

\paragraph{TCGA-UT.}
The dataset contains \num{1608060} patches from \num{8736} diagnostic slides across 32 cancer types \citep{tomczak2015reviewThe,komura2022universalEncoding}. Splits are assigned at the TCGA participant level (case-disjoint), using \num{5025} training, \num{1075} validation, and \num{1075} test cases per seed; all slides from one participant remain in one partition. Because every patch inherits its slide's cancer-type label, the patch and slide imbalance coincide ($\rho\approx\num{28}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.085}$). The patch benchmark caps each slide at 30 deterministic patches (empirical median of available tiles at resolution level~0), yielding a controlled manifest shared by all patch methods; the WSI-bag benchmark applies the same 30-instance cap to each slide's feature bag.

\paragraph{BRACS.}
Evidence is derived from the ROI set: each annotated ROI is tiled into $256\times256$ RGB patches, and each slide's pooled ROI tiles are capped at the empirical median (\num{360} tiles per slide). This design preserves label fidelity because all instances originate from pathologist-selected lesion regions. Of \num{547} published WSIs, \num{387} contribute at least one ROI tile; the benchmark covers \num{151} patients and \num{99352} extracted patches over the seven-subtype target, a mild imbalance ($\rho\approx\num{4.6}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.072}$ patch; $\rho\approx\num{2.3}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.018}$ slide).

\paragraph{PANDA.}
The WSI-bag regime uses the canonical six-class ISUP grade (0~=~benign, 1--5~=~increasing cancer grade groups; $\rho\approx\num{2.4}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.043}$). The patch regime uses a provider-consistent binary cancer/benign target derived from pixel masks ($\rho\approx\num{4.7}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.332}$); because Radboud and Karolinska masks use institution-dependent value semantics, collapsing both to cancer/benign is the only label the two providers share. To keep frozen-feature extraction tractable, the benchmark uses a stratified subset of approximately \num{2000} biopsies, sampled to preserve the joint distribution of ISUP grade and provider. The per-slide evidence budget is the empirical median number of tissue tiles per biopsy (\num{421}); biopsies without available masks are excluded from the patch regime but kept in the WSI-bag regime.

\subsection{Quantifying imbalance}
\label{sec:imbalance-measure}
Imbalance is reported primarily by the head-to-tail ratio, the imbalance factor standard to the
long-tailed-recognition literature the evaluated methods come from:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:imbalance-ratio}
  \rho \;=\; \frac{N_{\max}}{N_{\min}} \;\ge\; 1,
\end{equation}
the largest-to-smallest class support, with $\rho=1$ perfectly balanced and larger values more
skewed. Cui et al.\ and Buda et al.\ parameterise their long-tailed benchmarks directly by $\rho$
(imbalance factors up to \num{200}), so reporting $\rho$ places each dataset on the same axis as the
methods' design regime and as the controlled construction of \S\ref{sec:controlled}
\citep{cui2019classBalanced,buda2018systematic}.

The ratio has one blind spot: it depends only on the two extreme classes and ignores the shape of
the distribution between them, and it is not comparable across datasets with different class counts.
As a class-count-invariant cross-check, an entropy-based measure is therefore also reported. For
empirical class proportions $p_1,\dots,p_K$ (with $\sum_i p_i = 1$), the Shannon entropy
$H(p)=-\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \log p_i$ attains its maximum $\log K$ at the uniform distribution;
normalising and complementing gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:imbalance}
  \Hnorm \;=\; 1 - \frac{-\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \log p_i}{\log K} \;\in\; [0,1],
\end{equation}
with \num{0} perfectly balanced and values toward \num{1} more skewed. The $\log K$ normalisation
removes the dependence on the number of classes, so $\Hnorm$ compares a two-class cohort against a
thirty-two-class one on a common scale where $\rho$ cannot. Both are reported separately at patch
and slide level, because the two levels can differ sharply when patch labels are derived from masks
rather than inherited from the slide; the full native class distributions are given in
\citet{queisler2026datasets}.

\paragraph{The two measures answer different questions.}
Because $\Hnorm$ is a $\log K$-normalised average deviation from uniformity, a fixed $\rho$ maps to
very different $\Hnorm$ depending on $K$: CAMELYON16 patches ($\rho\approx\num{39}$, $K=2$) score
$\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$, whereas TCGA-UT patches at a comparable ratio ($\rho\approx\num{28}$,
$K=32$) score only $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.085}$. The reverse also holds: an artificially long-tailed
distribution at imbalance factor $\rho=100$---a regime everyone agrees is severe---still scores a
low $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.13}$ once it has a hundred classes, because entropy is then dominated by the
bulk of moderately populated classes and is nearly insensitive to how extreme the single rarest
class is. Neither behaviour is a defect: $\rho$ measures the extremity of the tail while $\Hnorm$
measures how far the \emph{whole} distribution sits from uniform, and the two coincide only at $K=2$.
Reading them together---$\rho$ as the headline, $\Hnorm$ as the $K$-invariant cross-check---avoids
being misled by either the tail-blindness of the entropy measure or the class-count-blindness of the
ratio.

\paragraph{Native imbalance is mild relative to the regime these methods target.}
At \emph{slide} level---the unit every diagnostic target in this benchmark is ultimately scored
on---all four datasets are only mildly skewed, whether read as $\Hnorm\in[\num{0.018},\num{0.085}]$
or as a head-to-tail ratio $\rho\le\num{28.3}$ (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}). The two large
$\Hnorm$ values in the table (CAMELYON16 patch $\num{0.832}$, PANDA patch $\num{0.332}$) are
within-slide, binary mask effects over modest underlying ratios ($\rho\approx\num{39}$ and
$\num{4.7}$), not properties of the typing, subtyping, or grading targets themselves. This matters
because the long-tailed-recognition methods evaluated here were designed for, and validated at, far
harsher imbalance: Cui et al.\ construct long-tailed benchmarks by exponential class-count decay at
imbalance factors $\rho=N_{\max}/N_{\min}$ up to \num{200} \citep{cui2019classBalanced}, and Buda et
al.\ study step and linear imbalance at comparable ratios \citep{buda2018systematic}.
Figure~\ref{fig:native-imbalance-regimes} places the native ratios against this
$\rho\in[\num{100},\num{200}]$ stress-test band: every slide-level target sits an order of magnitude
below it. Benchmarking imbalance-mitigation methods on the native distributions therefore
under-tests them, which motivates the controlled construction of \S\ref{sec:controlled}.

Macro F1 is the primary ranking metric within each regime because it weights classes equally and penalizes failure on rare classes; balanced accuracy and accuracy are reported alongside it. Three complementary probability-quality metrics---negative log likelihood (NLL), multiclass Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE)---are reported on held-out softmax outputs, both before and after a unified post-hoc temperature-scaling protocol in which, for each method and seed, a single temperature is fit on validation by minimizing NLL and applied unchanged to the test scores. Full metric definitions and the temperature-scaling protocol are given in \citet{queisler2026imbalanceMethods}.

RQ1 gives priority to tail-class recall and tail-class one-versus-rest AUROC alongside macro F1 and balanced accuracy, because aggregate gains can conceal deterioration in the rare classes that motivate the intervention. RQ2 reports calibration as a separate outcome rather than folding it into discrimination, and measures computational cost by training wall time, accelerator-hours, and peak accelerator memory relative to the corresponding CE run under the same training budget.

All four datasets use three random seeds, deterministic per-slide evidence caps, and frozen Virchow2 features. Hyperparameters are selected independently per method: for each candidate configuration the validation macro F1 is averaged over the three seeds and the configuration with the highest mean is chosen (balanced accuracy as tie-breaker); a single configuration per method is then fixed before evaluating on the held-out test split. Results are reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across seeds, and patch and WSI-bag methods are compared only within their own regime.

\subsection{Controlled imbalance construction}
\label{sec:controlled}
Section~\ref{sec:imbalance-measure} showed that every native target is only mildly imbalanced
relative to the long-tail stress-test regime the evaluated mitigation methods were designed for.
Rather than accepting that mismatch, each dataset can be restructured to a controlled, artificially
skewed distribution, applied \emph{independently} at patch and slide level to the class distribution
each regime is scored on. Three constructions from the long-tailed-recognition literature are
used---truncated exponential decay, step imbalance, and (for binary targets) ratio downsampling---each
enforcing a hard minimum per-class support $N_{\text{floor}}$ so that no class is starved below what
stable evaluation requires. The floor is grounded in the sampling precision of per-class recall.
Under a binomial approximation for recall estimated from $n$ test examples, the standard error
satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:recall-floor}
  \operatorname{SE}(\widehat{R})
  = \sqrt{\frac{R(1-R)}{n}}
  \leq \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}},
  \qquad
  N_{\text{floor}}(\varepsilon)
  = \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2 f_{\text{test}}},
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{test}}=\num{0.15}$ and $n=f_{\text{test}}N_{\text{floor}}$. Thus the patch floor
$N_{\text{floor}}=\num{200}$ yields approximately thirty test patches per class and a worst-case
$\operatorname{SE}\leq\num{0.091}$. The analogous precision target is infeasible for slides:
$N_{\text{floor}}=\num{20}$ yields approximately three test slides and the coarser bound
$\operatorname{SE}\leq\num{0.289}$, while remaining below the rarest retained TCGA-UT type
(DLBC, \num{28} slides). The two floors therefore follow the same standard-error formula but reflect
the different attainable precision at patch and slide level. The constructions only ever downsample.

For the causal contrasts in RQ1--RQ3, these target distributions serve as severity envelopes rather
than as unrelated training sets. Within each dataset and regime, a balanced CE condition and every
imbalanced condition use the same total training support $T$, the same patient split, and the same
unchanged validation and test sets. The largest feasible shared $T$ is chosen subject to the native
per-class support constraints at every compared severity; only the training allocation across classes
changes. Samples are nested where possible so that increasing imbalance removes examples from the
same common pool instead of changing case composition arbitrarily. Encoder, evidence budget,
optimisation schedule, and evaluation set remain fixed. Consequently, differences between balanced
and imbalanced CE estimate the effect of training imbalance rather than a mixture of imbalance and
training-set size.

\paragraph{Truncated exponential decay (Cui et al.).}
Ranking classes by native support, the target count for the $i$-th ranked class ($i=0,\dots,K-1$) is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:exp-decay}
  n_i \;=\; \max\bigl(N_{\text{floor}},\ \lfloor N_{\max}\,\mu^{\,i}\rfloor\bigr),
  \qquad \mu\in(0,1),
\end{equation}
with $\mu$ chosen to hit a target imbalance factor $\rho=N_{\max}/N_{\min}$ \citep{cui2019classBalanced}.
The floor caps the decay so that every class retains enough examples for macro-averaged evaluation
instead of thinning to a handful.

\paragraph{Step imbalance (Buda et al.).}
Classes are split into a head group, which keeps its native support, and a tail group, which is
uniformly downsampled to $N_{\text{floor}}$ \citep{buda2018systematic}. This isolates rarity as a
binary property (in the head group or not) rather than a continuous rank effect, and lets the tail
group be chosen for clinical relevance rather than by native rank. For binary targets neither
scheme applies, so the minority class is simply ratio-downsampled to a target $\rho$, floored. For
the multi-class exponential and step constructions, the achieved ratio is not an additional free
parameter: once the head retains its native support, it follows as
$\rho_{\text{target}}=N_{\max}^{\text{native}}/N_{\text{floor}}$. Binary patch targets instead need
an external anchor because their millions of majority patches would make that rule degenerate. They
use $\rho=\num{100}$, the entry of the $\rho\in[\num{100},\num{200}]$ regime in which the evaluated
methods were validated \citep{cui2019classBalanced,buda2018systematic}.

\paragraph{Per-dataset strategy.}
Table~\ref{tab:controlled-targets} lists the construction at both levels, in the order of
Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CAMELYON16} (binary at both levels). Slides are ratio-downsampled to a \num{10}:1
    normal:tumor prevalence (a low-prevalence screening scenario), which the small cohort caps near
    $\rho\approx\num{10}$; tumor patches, being abundant, are ratio-downsampled all the way to the
    stress regime ($\rho=\num{100}$).
  \item \textbf{TCGA-UT} (32 types, shared by both levels) uses exponential decay ranked by support.
    The slide target widens to $\rho\approx\num{40}$, but the patch target barely moves
    ($\rho\approx\num{28}$): the rarest type (DLBC) has only \num{168} patches, so the patch floor
    binds before the decay can steepen---an honest ceiling on how imbalanced the patch regime can be
    made without starving a class below evaluability.
  \item \textbf{BRACS} (7 subtypes, shared) uses step imbalance: the benign/usual head
    $\{$N, PB, UDH, IC$\}$ keeps native support while the clinically critical atypical/malignant tail
    $\{$FEA, ADH, DCIS$\}$ is downsampled to the floor, reaching $\rho\approx\num{10}$ (slide) and
    $\rho\approx\num{149}$ (patch).
  \item \textbf{PANDA} (6 ISUP grades on slides, binary cancer/benign on patches). The slide target
    uses exponential decay fixed to the ordinal grade order (respecting grade order) on the full
    \num{10616}-slide training cohort driving $\rho$ to $\approx\num{145}$; patches are ratio-downsampled to the
    stress regime ($\rho=\num{100}$).

For a discrimination metric $M$ oriented so that larger values are better, the imbalance-induced
deficit and the recovery fraction are defined as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:recovery}
  \begin{aligned}
    D_M
    &= M(\text{balanced CE})-M(\text{imbalanced CE}),\\
    R_M
    &= \frac{M(\text{method, imbalanced})-M(\text{imbalanced CE})}{D_M}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $R_M=0$ denotes no recovery, $R_M=1$ denotes recovery to the balanced-CE reference,
$R_M<0$ denotes further deterioration, and $R_M>1$ denotes improvement beyond that reference.
Recovery is reported only when $D_M$ is positive and distinguishable from seed variation; otherwise
the condition provides no evidenced imbalance deficit to recover. NLL, Brier score, and ECE retain
their natural lower-is-better orientation and are reported as paired changes, avoiding an opaque
composite of discrimination and calibration.

RQ3 uses predictors computed before any mitigation run. Feature-space separability is summarised by
balanced $k$-nearest-neighbour macro recall, a class-balanced linear probe, and per-class
nearest-neighbour error on frozen Virchow2 representations. Patch analyses operate on patch
embeddings; WSI analyses use a fixed, non-learned mean of the capped patch embeddings so that the
probe does not inherit the CE or MIL model being explained. Effective sample size is estimated per
class with the standard cluster correction
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:effective-sample-size}
  N_{\mathrm{eff},c}
  = \frac{N_c}{1+(\bar m_c-1)\operatorname{ICC}_c},
\end{equation}
where patches are clustered within patients/slides and multiple slides, where present, are clustered
within patients. Predictive analyses compare separability, $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, and $\rho$ against
$R_M$ without using CE test performance as a separability covariate.

CAMELYON16 provides a prespecified MIL-specific secondary analysis motivated by the distinction
between slide-level and instance-level imbalance in SC-MIL \citep{juyal2024scMil}. The number of
positive slides and the fraction of mask-positive instances retained within each tumor bag are varied
factorially while total slide support and bag size are fixed. This separates between-slide class
imbalance from within-slide informative-instance sparsity and tests their interaction on WSI-level
discrimination and calibration.

Three patterns motivate, rather than answer, the controlled research questions.

\emph{Native imbalance severity does not rank the observed gains.} CAMELYON16 has the highest patch-level imbalance in the suite ($\rho\approx\num{39}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.832}$) yet shows among the smallest mitigation deltas ($+\num{0.008}$ macro F1 at patch level). The largest positive deltas instead occur for BRACS patch ($\rho\approx\num{4.6}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.072}$, $\Delta=+\num{0.039}$) and PANDA WSI-bag ISUP grading ($\rho\approx\num{2.4}$, $\Hnorm\approx\num{0.043}$, $\Delta=+\num{0.025}$). Because task, support, and separability also differ across these datasets, this comparison is hypothesis-generating rather than a test of which property causes mitigation headroom.

\emph{Patch- and slide-level imbalance describe different mechanisms.} CAMELYON16's severe patch imbalance is largely a within-slide scarcity of tumor instances, whereas its slide labels are nearly balanced ($\Hnorm\approx\num{0.028}$). A single ratio therefore cannot represent both rare positive bags and sparse informative instances; the factorial CAMELYON16 analysis separates these mechanisms directly.

\emph{Discrimination and calibration vary together in the native runs.} Near-ceiling regimes reach post-scaling ECE near \num{0.001} (CAMELYON16 patch), whereas the grading and subtyping regimes retain residual post-scaling ECE up to \num{0.10}--\num{0.17}. The independently computed probes in RQ3 test whether frozen-feature separability explains this pattern better than $\rho$ or effective sample size; the CE test score itself is not used as explanatory evidence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{(a) Frozen-encoder bottleneck.}
Once a strong frozen pathology encoder is fixed, patch-level gains come from reshaping the decision head or objective rather than from resampling or reweighting. CFAL replaces the linear head with learnable class prototypes and a margin objective, pulling examples toward compact class centres and penalising confusion with nearby wrong-class prototypes. OKO constructs training sets with one matched pair and $k$ distractors; averaging per-example logits across set members implicitly regularises logit magnitude and suppresses the over-confidence that afflicts CE-trained classifiers, which explains its combination of high balanced accuracy and the best raw NLL and Brier among patch methods. The simpler reweighting and resampling variants remain within the CE overlap band, consistent with the interpretation that exposure corrections address only part of the error pattern when the representation already separates most classes. ProGAN augmentation does not deliver a clear advantage despite pixel-level FID comparable to the source study \citep{ruizcasado2024enhancingHistopathological}; the decisive difference is that the source method trains its classifier end-to-end on pixels, whereas this benchmark feeds synthetic patches through a frozen encoder that may map slightly off-manifold synthetic patches into embedding regions the head rarely sees for real data. On WSI bags, RankMix and MDE-MIL improve on plain MIL CE in every seed, because the bottleneck there includes instance selection and bag aggregation, which mixing informative subsequences (RankMix) and coupling natural and balanced branches with cross-expert consistency (MDE-MIL) can address even when the underlying features are strong.

\paragraph{(b) Candidate predictors of recovery.}
The native runs motivate class separability as a candidate predictor but do not establish it as the governing cause. BRACS is mildly imbalanced yet difficult, whereas CAMELYON16 is severely imbalanced at patch level but near ceiling; PANDA likewise presents detection and grading targets of different difficulty on the same biopsies. These contrasts confound imbalance with task, support, and prediction unit, and baseline performance mechanically limits the possible gain over CE. The controlled deficits and recovery fractions in Equation~\ref{eq:recovery}, together with the independent feature-space probes and effective-sample-size estimates, provide the non-circular test required to compare these explanations.

\paragraph{(c) ProGAN frozen-feature hypothesis.}
ProGAN augmentation is informative on BRACS and PANDA: the mild native imbalances never create the tail-scarcity regime where synthetic minority augmentation helped on TCGA-UT at harsher constructed severities. On BRACS (BAcc \num{0.460} versus CE \num{0.451}) and PANDA (macro F1 \num{0.855} versus CE \num{0.859}), ProGAN lands within between-seed noise of the CE baseline, neither helping nor hurting. This is consistent with the frozen-feature explanation rather than a generator-quality failure: the generators reach reasonable pixel-level FID, but their outputs must pass through a frozen encoder that may map slightly off-manifold synthetic patches into embedding regions the head rarely sees for real data.

\paragraph{(d) Limitations.}
TCGA-UT patch labels come from pathologist-selected tumor regions; they are useful patch-level class labels but not dense annotations. Within each dataset the two benchmarks differ in prediction unit and per-slide evidence budget, so their result tables are non-transferable. The validation sweep varies one imbalance-specific control per method and does not exhaust architecture or training-schedule choices, and both regimes use frozen features rather than end-to-end encoder adaptation; the benchmark is controlled rather than a full reproduction of each original paper. Calibration results for the BRACS and PANDA WSI-bag regimes are additionally affected by small test sets and fine-grained label sets rather than method-specific weaknesses. Most importantly, the reported native-distribution results lack the size-matched balanced CE condition required for $D_M$ and $R_M$; they therefore cannot by themselves answer RQ1--RQ3. Those questions apply to the controlled follow-up defined in \S\ref{sec:controlled}.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================
This report benchmarked representative class-imbalance mitigation methods on four histopathology datasets using frozen Virchow2 features, bounded evidence budgets, and patient-disjoint splits. The completed native-distribution runs show heterogeneous method gains and calibration across patch and WSI supervision, but they do not isolate imbalance-induced damage. The controlled design therefore first measures the balanced-to-imbalanced CE deficit, then expresses mitigation performance as the fraction of that deficit recovered while reporting calibration and computational costs separately. Independent separability probes, cluster-adjusted effective sample size, and head-to-tail ratio are compared as predictors of recovery, with an additional CAMELYON16 analysis separating rare positive slides from sparse informative instances. This framing distinguishes general task difficulty from damage caused specifically by training imbalance and prevents a mechanically low CE baseline from being interpreted as mitigation headroom.

\section{Terminology}
\label{app:terminology}

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.23\linewidth}p{0.68\linewidth}}
    \toprule
    Term & Meaning in this study\\
    \midrule
    \emph{Slide / WSI} & Diagnostic whole-slide image carrying one label.\\
    \emph{Case / patient} & TCGA participant identified by the first three fields of the TCGA slide barcode (e.g.\ \texttt{TCGA-OR-A5J1}); or the equivalent patient identifier in BRACS and PANDA.\\
    \emph{Patch} & $256\times256$ H\&E crop from a selected region; patch labels inherit the slide-level class.\\
    \emph{Patch embedding} & Frozen Virchow2 vector for one controlled patch; input to the patch-level classifier head.\\
    \emph{WSI bag} & Set of patch-feature instances for one slide, consumed by the MIL model.\\
    \emph{Imbalance factor $\rho$} & Head-to-tail ratio $N_{\max}/N_{\min}\ge 1$ (the long-tailed-recognition imbalance factor); primary imbalance measure, reported at patch and slide level.\\
    \emph{Imbalance $\Hnorm$} & Entropy-based class-imbalance measure in $[0,1]$ (\num{0} balanced, \num{1} maximally skewed); class-count-invariant cross-check to $\rho$, reported at patch and slide level \citep{queisler2026datasets}.\\
    \emph{Head/body/tail class} & Fixed support tier from the full TCGA-UT slide distribution (8 each for head and tail; 16 body).\\
    \emph{Case-disjoint split} & Split rule preventing any participant's slides, patches, or features from crossing train, validation, and test.\\
    \emph{Evidence budget} & Per-slide cap on the number of patch or bag instances; dataset-specific, following a common data-driven median rule (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview}).\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Terminology used across all four dataset benchmarks.}
  \label{tab:dataset-terminology}
\end{table}
