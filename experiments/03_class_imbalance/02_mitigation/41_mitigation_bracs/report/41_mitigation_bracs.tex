\begin{abstract}
\noindent A frozen Virchow2 encoder with a logistic-regression readout loses 7.65 points of balanced accuracy on BRACS when the training classes are imbalanced at a ratio of 100. This report asks how much of that loss six established mitigation methods recover when the encoder adapts through low-rank updates (LoRA). On 30 patient-disjoint split-draw shards, a cross-entropy (CE) model is trained on a balanced class allocation, and CE and every method are trained on allocations with ratios 50 and 100 and with BRACS's native class shares; each method's strength is selected per shard on validation patients. Under LoRA, CE loses 4.95 points at ratio 100 and 3.54 points at ratio 50, while its 0.85-point loss at native shares cannot be distinguished from zero. At ratio 100, a curriculum of data augmentation (CUDA) gives the most accurate model, 2.59 points above CE, or about half of the loss. Train-time and post-hoc logit adjustment follow at about two-fifths and are the only methods that substantially raise recall of the rarest classes. No method closes the gap. Fitting the methods on the balanced allocation as well shows how they behave without imbalance: CUDA improves the balanced model by 1.39 points, so most of its gain does not depend on imbalance, whereas GCL and cRT lower balanced accuracy slightly and are less affected by imbalance than their gain over CE suggests. Except for CUDA, methods that re-fit only the classifier on a frozen encoder recover as much as methods trained end to end. The smaller loss under LoRA reflects a balanced model about three points less accurate than the frozen readout, not a more robust imbalanced one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
When the patient support per class is held fixed, class imbalance alone reduces balanced accuracy on BRACS by 7.65 points at an imbalance ratio of $\rho=100$, against 2.91 points on TCGA-UT, for a logistic-regression readout on frozen Virchow2 features \citep{queisler2026prevalenceBracs,queisler2026prevalenceTcga}. On BRACS, this loss splits into two effects of similar size \citep{queisler2026causeBracs}. In the \emph{frequency} effect, the common classes dominate the training loss and pull the decision boundary toward themselves. In the \emph{support} effect, the rare classes offer too few distinct patches to describe how they vary, which no reweighting can supply. These analyses keep the encoder frozen, so they do not test the established long-tailed methods that also shape the representation.

Six such methods have been defined for patch classification under a Virchow2 encoder adapted only through LoRA \citep{queisler2026mitigationMethods}: balanced sampling and a curriculum of data augmentation (CUDA) at the data level, logit adjustment and DisAlign at the algorithm level, and classifier re-training (cRT) and Gaussian clouded logit (GCL) adjustment as hybrids. This report asks: \emph{once the encoder can adapt, how much of BRACS's imbalance loss does each method recover, and does recovery differ between methods trained end to end and methods that freeze the encoder and re-fit only the classifier?}

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
The shards follow the imbalance design of \citet{queisler2026prevalenceBracs}. BRACS has three fixed patient-disjoint splits, each with about 103 training, 24 validation, and 24 test patients, and each split is drawn ten times. A draw samples $G=10$ training patients per lesion class from those with at least 160 training patches and holds a total budget of $T=7\cdot G\cdot 32=2{,}240$ patches fixed across all allocations. Every allocation of a draw uses the same patients and the same random assignment of classes to allocation ranks, so the 30 split-draw shards differ between allocations only in the class shares. Training reads decoded patch images, because every method adapts the encoder: a rank-8 LoRA update on the attention projections plus a linear head (a cosine head for GCL), as defined by \citet{queisler2026mitigationMethods}.

\subsection{Allocations and methods}
Each shard's patch budget is divided among the seven classes in four ways, following an exponential allocation profile. The \emph{balanced} allocation ($\rho=1$) trains CE, which serves as the \emph{undamaged reference}, and the methods whose effect there is not zero by construction (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Three imbalanced allocations, with ratios $\rho=50$ and $\rho=100$ and with BRACS's native class shares (N, realized ratio about 6.7), each train CE as their \emph{damaged reference} and every mitigation method over the grid of Table~\ref{tab:grid}. The two ratios show whether recovery grows with severity; the native shares ask whether mitigation matters at the imbalance BRACS actually has.

Ratio 100 is close to the most severe imbalance this design can express. At $\rho=100$ the rarest class receives about 12 of the 2,240 patches, roughly one per patient. Beyond $\rho\approx125$ it would receive fewer patches than its ten patients, so the tail class could no longer draw on all its patients and the design would mix imbalance with patient shortage.

Methods fall into two training families. \emph{End-to-end} methods (balanced sampling, CUDA, train-time logit adjustment, GCL) train the LoRA update and head together. CUDA \citep{ahn2023cuda} augments image patches before the encoder, with a per-class strength that rises as the current model learns the class, so it can only act end to end. \emph{Classifier-only} methods freeze an end-to-end model's encoder and re-fit only classifier parameters: post-hoc logit adjustment, DisAlign, and cRT start from the allocation's CE model, and GCL's classifier-only form, its second training stage, starts from the end-to-end GCL model of the same $\sigma$. Logit adjustment and GCL therefore each appear once per family, giving eight method variants.

The strength grids bracket the defaults of the original methods. Balanced sampling and CUDA's augmentation probability end at their maximum of 1, and train-time $\tau$ at the full prior correction $\tau=1$. The other grids extend past any edge on which the validation selection would otherwise concentrate, which at native shares widens the GCL and DisAlign grids.

\subsection{Training length and fit count}
End-to-end training length was fixed in advance on CE alone. CE was trained on the balanced and the $\rho=100$ allocation, one draw per split, for 150, 300, 600, and 1,200 steps, and the rule selects the smallest step count within 0.5 points of the best mean validation balanced accuracy at $\rho=100$. The four candidates reached 31.87, 34.22, 34.93, and 33.42 percent, so 600 steps (batch 256, learning rate $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule) were used for all end-to-end fits; classifier-only fits train 200 steps. One length serves all allocations, so that a recovery difference between allocations cannot come from training length. It was chosen at the most severe allocation and not re-tuned at $\rho=50$ or native shares. Per shard, the grid holds 39 end-to-end and 41 classifier-only configurations, for 1,170 and 1,230 fits over the 30 shards. Every configured fit completed and entered the analysis.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
The primary endpoint is test patient-macro balanced accuracy (BA), the mean of per-class patch recalls in which every test patient of a class carries equal weight. Estimates pool the 30 shards with equal weight per split. Uncertainty combines Bayesian bootstrap weights on test patients \citep{rubin1981bayesian} with resampling of draws within each split over 2,000 replicates. All allocations and methods share the replicate weights, so every contrast is paired; intervals are 95 percent percentile intervals.

For each method, shard, and imbalanced allocation, the grid value with the best \emph{validation} BA is selected; the test partition is never read for selection. With $m^{*}$ the selected fit and $a$ the allocation,
\begin{equation}
  D_a=\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},a),\qquad
  R_m=\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},a),\qquad
  \text{share}_m=R_m/D_a ,
  \label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
\noindent where the share is computed per replicate. A recovery counts as \emph{material} if $R_m$ is at least one point and its interval lies above zero, and as \emph{full} if the interval of $\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)$ contains the point estimate of $\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1)$.

$R_m$ compares a method with CE, so it mixes two effects: a correction of the imbalance and any general effect the method has on accuracy. To separate the two, the methods are also fitted on the balanced allocation with the same validation selection. A method's \emph{balanced gain} and its \emph{imbalance-specific recovery} are
\begin{equation}
  G_m=\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},1)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1),\qquad
  R_m-G_m=D_a-\bigl[\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},1)-\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)\bigr],
  \label{eq:specific}
\end{equation}
\noindent the difference between the damage CE suffers and the damage the method suffers. At equal class counts, balanced sampling and both forms of logit adjustment are identical to CE, so $G_m=0$ for them by construction and they need no balanced fits. GCL's cloud size and DisAlign's reweighting are also uniform there, so their strengths have no effect and one value each is fitted; GCL then reduces to CE with a cosine head.

Secondary readings are descriptive: head, body, and tail recall by assigned class rank; macro negative log-likelihood (NLL) and expected calibration error (ECE), raw and after validation temperature scaling; the frequency of each selected strength; and the mean share of the end-to-end and classifier-only families. The damage of the frozen readout \citep{queisler2026prevalenceBracs} uses the same test patients but a different classifier, so it serves as an external reference without a paired interval.
