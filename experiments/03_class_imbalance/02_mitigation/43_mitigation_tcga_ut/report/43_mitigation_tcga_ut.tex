\begin{abstract}
\noindent On BRACS, six established mitigation methods recovered at most about half of the balanced accuracy lost under severe class imbalance with a Virchow2 encoder adapted through low-rank adaptation (LoRA). This report repeats that study on TCGA-UT, a 30-class dataset on which imbalance costs a frozen readout only 2.91 percentage points at a ratio of 100. The study uses 30 patient-disjoint split-draw shards and a balanced cross-entropy (CE) reference. CE and every mitigation method also train at imbalance ratios of 50 and 100 and at native class shares. Validation patients determine each method's strength per shard. Under LoRA, CE loses 1.56 points at ratio 100, 0.67 points at ratio 50, and 0.42 points at native shares: between a third and a half of the frozen readout's losses. At ratio 100, logit adjustment removes the loss: its train-time form recovers 1.54 points and its post-hoc form 1.41 points, and the intervals for both recovery shares include 1. Classifier-only Gaussian clouded logit (GCL) adjustment recovers 1.13 points. Logit adjustment and classifier-only GCL are also the only methods that restore most of the rarest classes' recall. A curriculum of data augmentation (CUDA) recovers 1.02 points but also improves the balanced model by 0.81 points, so its gain does not depend on imbalance. At native shares, it is the only method that gains a full point. After subtracting their slightly negative balanced gains, classifier-only GCL, end-to-end GCL, and classifier re-training (cRT) recover 1.73, 1.42, and 1.10 points of imbalance-specific loss. At ratio 100, a prior correction at inference therefore recovers nearly all of the loss, and differences between methods remain below one point.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
With patient support per class fixed, class imbalance reduces balanced accuracy (BA) by 2.91 percentage points on TCGA-UT and 7.65 points on BRACS at an imbalance ratio of $\rho=100$, using a logistic-regression readout on frozen Virchow2 features \citep{queisler2026prevalenceTcga,queisler2026prevalenceBracs}. On TCGA-UT, two sources of loss compound \citep{queisler2026causeTcga}. In the \emph{frequency} effect, common classes dominate the training loss and pull the decision boundary toward themselves. In the \emph{support} effect, rare classes offer too few distinct patches to capture their variation. These effects alone cost 1.20 and 0.53 points, respectively, at $\rho=100$; their interaction costs a further 1.18 points.

A companion study measured how much of BRACS's loss six established long-tailed methods recover when the Virchow2 encoder \citep{zimmermann2024virchow2} uses low-rank adaptation (LoRA) \citep{queisler2026mitigationBracs,queisler2026mitigationMethods}. There, cross-entropy (CE) training lost 4.95 points at $\rho=100$. A curriculum of data augmentation (CUDA) recovered about half of that loss and logit adjustment about two-fifths; no method closed the gap. That study covered one dataset. TCGA-UT differs from BRACS in several respects relevant to mitigation: it has 30 classes instead of seven, a balanced accuracy near 72 instead of 37 percent, and an imbalance loss less than half as large. This report asks: \emph{how much of TCGA-UT's imbalance loss does each method recover under a LoRA-adapted encoder, and do the conclusions drawn on BRACS carry over to a dataset on which imbalance costs little?}

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
The shards follow the imbalance design of \citet{queisler2026prevalenceTcga}. The 30 TCGA-UT cancer types have three fixed patient-disjoint splits, each sampled in ten draws. Each draw samples $G=20$ training patients per cancer type from those with at least 160 training patches. The total patch budget, $T=30\cdot G\cdot 32=19{,}200$, remains fixed across allocations. Within each of the 30 split-draw shards, all allocations use the same patients and the same random assignment of classes to allocation ranks; only the class shares differ. Validation and test partitions retain their natural class distribution. Training uses decoded patch images because every method adapts the encoder through a rank-8 LoRA update on the attention projections, with a linear head (a cosine head for Gaussian clouded logit, GCL, adjustment), as defined by \citet{queisler2026mitigationMethods}.

\subsection{Allocations}
Each shard's patch budget is divided among the 30 classes in four ways, following an exponential allocation profile. The \emph{balanced} allocation ($\rho=1$) trains CE as the \emph{undamaged reference}, along with methods whose effect on balanced data is not zero by construction (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Three imbalanced allocations use ratios $\rho=50$ and $\rho=100$ and TCGA-UT's native class shares (N, realized ratio about 18.1). Each trains CE as its \emph{damaged reference} and every mitigation method over the grid in Table~\ref{tab:grid}. At $\rho=100$, the largest class receives 2,843 patches and the rarest 28, between one and two per patient. This ratio approaches the most severe imbalance the design can express without removing patients from the tail.

Methods fall into two training families. \emph{End-to-end} methods (balanced sampling, CUDA, train-time logit adjustment, GCL) train the LoRA update and head together. CUDA \citep{ahn2023cuda} augments image patches before the encoder, with a per-class strength that rises as the model learns the class, so it acts only end to end. \emph{Classifier-only} methods freeze an end-to-end model's encoder and adjust only classifier parameters. Post-hoc logit adjustment, distribution alignment (DisAlign), and classifier re-training (cRT) start from the allocation's CE model. GCL's classifier-only form, its second training stage, starts from the end-to-end GCL model with the same $\sigma$. Logit adjustment and GCL therefore each appear in both families, giving eight method variants.

\subsection{Training length}
End-to-end training length was fixed in advance using CE alone. CE models trained on the balanced and $\rho=100$ allocations, with one draw per split, for 300, 600, 1,200, 2,400, and 4,800 steps. The selection rule chose the smallest step count within 0.5 points of the best mean validation BA at $\rho=100$. The five candidates reached 59.9, 65.3, 69.1, 69.8, and 69.7 percent, respectively. On the balanced allocation, doubling from 2,400 to 4,800 steps raised validation BA by only 0.2 points, from 71.4 to 71.6 percent. All end-to-end fits therefore used 2,400 steps (batch size 256, learning rate $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule), about 32 passes over the 19,200 training patches; classifier-only fits used 200 steps. Training length remains fixed across allocations within each family, so it cannot explain differences in recovery between allocations. Per shard, the grid contains 43 end-to-end and 45 classifier-only configurations, giving 1,290 and 1,350 fits across the 30 shards. Every configured fit completed and entered the analysis.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
The primary endpoint is test patient-macro BA: the mean of per-class patch recalls, with equal weight for every test patient within a class. Estimates pool the 30 shards with equal weight per split. Across 2,000 replicates, uncertainty combines Bayesian bootstrap weights on test patients \citep{rubin1981bayesian} with resampling of draws within each split. All allocations and methods share the replicate weights, so every contrast is paired. Reported intervals are 95 percent percentile intervals.

For each method, shard, and imbalanced allocation, the grid value with the best \emph{validation} BA is selected; selection never uses the test partition. With $m^{*}$ denoting the selected fit and $a$ the allocation,
\begin{equation}
  D_a=\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},a),\qquad
  R_m=\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},a),\qquad
  \text{share}_m=R_m/D_a ,
  \label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
\noindent where the share is computed per replicate. Recovery counts as \emph{material} if $R_m$ is at least one point and its interval lies above zero. It counts as \emph{full} if the interval for $\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)$ contains the point estimate of $\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1)$. Because that interval is unpaired, the paired share better indicates how much damage remains.

$R_m$ compares a method with CE, combining imbalance correction with any general effect on accuracy. To separate these effects, the methods also train on the balanced allocation with the same validation selection. A method's \emph{balanced gain} and \emph{imbalance-specific recovery} are
\begin{equation}
  G_m=\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},1)-\mathrm{BA}(\mathrm{CE},1),\qquad
  R_m-G_m=D_a-\bigl[\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},1)-\mathrm{BA}(m^{*},a)\bigr],
  \label{eq:specific}
\end{equation}
\noindent with the latter measuring the difference between the damage to CE and the damage to the method. At equal class counts, balanced sampling and both forms of logit adjustment are identical to CE, so $G_m=0$ by construction and they need no balanced fits. GCL's cloud size and DisAlign's reweighting are also uniform under balanced allocation. Their strengths therefore have no effect, and each uses one value; GCL then reduces to CE with a cosine head.

Secondary analyses are descriptive: head, body, and tail recall by assigned class rank at $\rho=100$; macro negative log-likelihood (NLL) and expected calibration error (ECE), raw and after validation temperature scaling; the frequency of each selected strength; and the mean recovery share for each training family. The frozen-readout damage \citep{queisler2026prevalenceTcga} uses the same shards and test patients but a different classifier, so it serves as an external reference without a paired interval. Comparisons with BRACS \citep{queisler2026mitigationBracs} are likewise descriptive.
