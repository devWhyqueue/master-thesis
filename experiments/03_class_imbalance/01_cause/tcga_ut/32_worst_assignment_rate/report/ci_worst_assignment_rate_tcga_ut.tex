\begin{abstract}
\noindent At an imbalance ratio of $\rho=100$ on TCGA-UT, a random assignment of the 30 cancer types to the ranks of a long-tailed training allocation costs 2.91 points of test balanced accuracy. Earlier studies of this series traced the dependence of that damage on the assignment to pairs of classes the balanced classifier confuses, but a first attempt to turn this into a score over class orders failed its own pre-registered check. This report repairs that score with one change: each confused pair is weighted by the absolute rate at which the balanced classifier sends one class to the other, instead of by that pair's share of the class's errors. The repaired score passes the same check on damages already observed, and a calibration line fitted on 30 random orders predicts both sorted orders of the companion tail-assignment study inside their confidence intervals. Three class orders derived from the score were then fitted, 30 fits each. The order that maximizes the score costs 4.51 points, 1.60 points (95 percent interval 1.11 to 2.08) more than a random order; the order that minimizes it costs 1.34 points, 1.57 points (1.11 to 2.03) less. Reversing the worst order, which keeps every confused pair exactly as far apart in rank but swaps which member sits at the smaller allocation, costs 1.11 points (0.47 to 1.73) less than the worst order, so the direction of separation matters and not only its size. The calibration line, fixed before these fits, predicts all three within 0.37 points. At a fixed ratio and a fixed budget, the choice of which classes are rare therefore moves the damage over a range of 3.2 points, larger than the average damage of the ratio itself.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
The TCGA-UT prevalence study measured how balanced accuracy on 30 cancer types degrades as the training class distribution moves from balanced to long-tailed, at twenty patients per class and 19,200 training patches held fixed \citep{queisler2026prevalenceTcga}. At $\rho=100$ the damage was 2.91 points, averaged over class orders drawn at random per fit. The tail-assignment study then fixed two orders, sorting the classes by their recall under balanced training and placing either the easiest or the hardest classes in the tail \citep{queisler2026tailTcga}. Both cost less than a random order, 2.41 and 1.59 points, and no class collapsed. What moved a class's recall was its allocation relative to the classes it is confused with: rectum adenocarcinoma lost 13 points of recall while sitting just below colon adenocarcinoma. That study named the natural worst case, an assignment that separates strongly confused pairs and puts the member with more recall to lose underneath, but did not fit it.

The worst-assignment study wrote that candidate as a directed pair-separation score and checked the score against the three damages already known before spending any fit on it \citep{queisler2026worstTcga}. The score failed. It ranked the easy-tail order above every random order, although the easy-tail order is milder than the random average. The report traced the failure to the weights. Each class's confusion weights were normalized to sum to one, so a class that the balanced classifier almost never gets wrong carried as much total weight as a class it confuses constantly. Sorting the classes by recall then aligned the remaining weight, recall itself, with rank, and the score rewarded that alignment rather than the separation of confused pairs.

This report keeps the score and the search of the worst-assignment study and changes only the weight: a pair now enters with the absolute rate at which the balanced classifier predicts one class for the other. It asks whether the order that maximizes the repaired score is more damaging than a random order, whether the order that minimizes it is milder, and whether the direction of separation, not only its size, contributes. Section~\ref{sec:design} states the score, the orders it produces, and the arms fitted; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} states the gate and the pre-registered endpoints; the results follow in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
The study reuses the design of the TCGA-UT prevalence curve \citep{queisler2026prevalenceTcga}: 30 cancer types with frozen Virchow2 features \citep{zimmermann2024virchow2}, three patient-disjoint splits with ten draws each, $G=20$ patients per class, a total budget of $T=19{,}200$ training patches, and nested patch orderings within each patient. The balanced arm r1 gives each class 640 patches. The ratio arm r$\rho$ gives the class at rank $j$ a count proportional to $\rho^{-j/29}$, so at $\rho=100$ the counts fall from 2,843 patches at rank 0 to 28 patches at rank 29. Only the assignment of classes to ranks varies between arms; patients, patch orderings, and per-rank counts are held fixed, so any difference in damage between two arms is due to the assignment alone. The classifier is multinomial logistic regression with regularization selected on validation patient-macro balanced accuracy \citep{queisler2026patientShortage}.

\subsection{A rate-weighted pair-separation score}
\label{sec:score}
Each rank $r$ holds $n_r$ training patches, and the score measures allocations on a logarithmic scale relative to the balanced arm, $z_r = \log_2(n_r/640)$. The quantity $z_r$ is not a patch count. It counts how many times the allocation at rank $r$ doubles or halves the balanced 640 patches, so it is positive for ranks that hold more than 640 patches and negative for ranks that hold fewer. At $\rho=100$, rank 0 holds 2,843 patches, $z_0 = \log_2(2843/640) = 2.15$, and rank 29 holds 28 patches, $z_{29} = \log_2(28/640) = -4.51$. A difference $z_{r_d} - z_{r_c}$ is therefore the number of doublings by which the allocation of class $d$ exceeds that of class $c$. An assignment $\pi$ places class $c$ at rank $r_c$. The score is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  S(\pi) = \sum_{c \neq d}\;
  \underbrace{w_{c \rightarrow d}}_{\substack{\text{how often } c\\ \text{is predicted as } d}}\;
  \underbrace{h_c}_{\substack{\text{recall } c\\ \text{can lose}}}\;
  \underbrace{\max\!\left(z_{r_d} - z_{r_c},\, 0\right)}_{\substack{\text{doublings by which } d\text{'s}\\ \text{allocation exceeds } c\text{'s}}},
  \\[4pt]
  w_{c \rightarrow d} = \frac{N_{cd}}{\sum_{e} N_{ce}},
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $N_{cd}$ counts the test patches of class $c$ that the balanced arm predicts as class $d$, pooled over all fits, and $h_c$ is class $c$'s pooled balanced-arm recall in percent. The weight $w_{c \rightarrow d}$ is thus the probability that the balanced classifier predicts $d$ for a patch of class $c$. Each term measures the exposure of class $c$ to a class $d$ it is mistaken for that holds a larger allocation, scaled by how much recall $c$ has to lose.

Figure~\ref{fig:intuition} illustrates single terms with rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) as class $c$. READ is often predicted as colon adenocarcinoma (COAD). With READ at rank 27 and COAD at rank 2, COAD holds 5.73 doublings more patches, and the term is large (panel a). The term is small when the two classes sit at adjacent ranks (panel b). It is zero when READ sits above COAD, because the $\max$ keeps only the direction in which the confusion partner holds more patches (panel c); the reverse term, COAD predicted as READ, then becomes active instead, with its own weight and recall. The term is also near zero when READ sits far below a class it is almost never predicted as, such as thyroid carcinoma (THCA), because $w_{c \rightarrow d}$ is then close to zero (panel d). A high-scoring pair is thus a pair that the balanced classifier confuses often, placed far apart, with the more often confused member at the smaller allocation.

The score of a whole assignment adds these terms over all 870 ordered pairs of classes. An assignment scores high when many strongly confused pairs are separated at the same time, each with the right direction; one assignment can satisfy many pairs at once because each pair constrains only the relative ranks of its two members. The bottom panels of Figure~\ref{fig:intuition} show the result for the two extreme orders of Section~\ref{sec:arms}. In the worst order, nearly every class sits far below the class it is most often predicted as. In the mild order, most confused classes sit next to their partner, and the remaining gaps are short.

The formula is that of the worst-assignment study; only the denominator of $w$ differs \citep{queisler2026worstTcga}. There it was the off-diagonal row sum $\sum_{e\neq c} N_{ce}$, so every class carried total weight one. With the full row sum in the denominator, a class's weights sum to its patch-level error rate, and a class that is rarely confused contributes little no matter where it sits. The score remains directed: because the per-rank counts are log-linear in rank, reversing an order preserves every pairwise rank gap, and only the one-sided $\max$ and the asymmetric weights $w_{c \rightarrow d} h_c$ distinguish an order from its reversal.

\subsection{Derived orders and fitted arms}
\label{sec:arms}
The score is maximized and minimized over the $30!$ assignments by pairwise-swap hill climbing from 50 seeded random starts, taking the best improving swap until no swap improves. The result is an extreme assignment found by local search, not a proven optimum. Three orders follow. The \emph{worst} order maximizes $S$. The \emph{mild} order minimizes it. The \emph{flip} order reverses the worst order; it separates confused pairs exactly as far as the worst order does but swaps which member of each pair holds the larger allocation, which isolates the directional part of the claim from the separation itself.

In the worst order, colon adenocarcinoma sits at rank 2 and rectum adenocarcinoma at rank 27; lung squamous cell carcinoma sits at rank 0, with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma at rank 19 and cervical squamous cell carcinoma at rank 29; the lower-grade glioma sits at rank 5 and glioblastoma at rank 24. The mild order places each of these pairs at adjacent or near-adjacent ranks. Across the worst order, rank and balanced-arm recall correlate at a Spearman coefficient of only 0.08, so the order is not a disguised sort by difficulty, which was the failure mode of the worst-assignment study.

Each of the three orders was fitted at $\rho=100$ on all 30 split-draw combinations, 90 fits in total, with the same patients and patch orderings as every other arm. The r1, r10, r100 and native-distribution arms of the prevalence curve and the easy-tail and hard-tail arms of the tail-assignment study are reused unchanged \citep{queisler2026prevalenceTcga, queisler2026tailTcga}.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
The \emph{damage} $D$ of an arm is its loss of test patient-macro balanced accuracy against r1, in points. Pooling and uncertainty follow the prevalence curve: equal weight per split, Bayesian bootstrap weights on test patients \citep{rubin1981bayesian} combined with resampling of draws within splits, and 95 percent percentile intervals of every paired contrast from 2,000 replicates.

\subsection{Gate and calibration line}
\label{sec:gate}
Before any of the 90 fits, the repaired score was checked with the same gate that the worst-assignment study fixed \citep{queisler2026worstTcga}. G1 requires $S(\text{easy}) > S(\text{hard})$; G2 requires the mean of $S$ over the 30 random orders of the prevalence curve to exceed $S(\text{easy})$; G3, descriptive, reports the Spearman correlation between $S$ and single-fit damage across those random orders. In addition, a straight line of $D$ on $S$ was fitted to the 30 random orders alone and used to predict the damage of the easy-tail and hard-tail orders, which the line does not see, and of the three derived orders. These predictions were recorded before submission and are not refitted afterwards.

Single-fit damages are noisy. Across the 30 random orders the damage has a standard deviation of 0.70 points, and fixed orders refitted on all 30 split-draw combinations still vary by 0.47 to 0.67 points per fit. Only about a third of the per-fit variance is due to the order, which caps the Spearman coefficient that even a perfect score could reach against single random fits at about 0.57.

\subsection{Pre-registered endpoints}
\label{sec:endpoints}
Three contrasts were fixed before submission, each judged by whether its 95 percent interval excludes zero and against the one-point practical threshold of the series:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1} (primary): $D_{\text{worst}} - D_{\text{r100}} > 0$, where $D_{\text{r100}}$ is the random-order damage of the prevalence curve.
  \item \textbf{H2}: $D_{\text{r100}} - D_{\text{mild}} > 0$.
  \item \textbf{H3} (direction): $D_{\text{worst}} - D_{\text{flip}} > 0$. The line predicted a difference of 0.66 points, which the design might not resolve.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Two descriptive analyses complete the evaluation: the observed against the predicted damage of all five fixed orders, and the per-class recall change for the main confused pairs.

The score deserves a caveat stated before the results. It was chosen after exploratory checks on stored outputs, from about nine variants of the weight and direction terms, and its agreement with the easy-tail and hard-tail anchors is therefore in-sample in the sense that matters: those anchors informed the choice. It differs from the failed score by one change with a clear rationale, and three variants that weight pairs symmetrically or by row share produced worst and mild orders of similar predicted damage. The three derived orders were never fitted before the choice was fixed, and they are the real test.
