\begin{abstract}
\begingroup
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}
\noindent A class can be underserved for several distinct reasons, and the count that marks it as rare does not say which. This report presents the outcomes of the controlled patch-classification benchmark specified in the companion protocol \citep{queisler2026benchmarkProtocol} on BRACS, a seven-class breast-lesion task evaluated with frozen Virchow2 features. The design crosses two axes that ordinary imbalance confounds. Along the \emph{nominal} axis a fixed patch budget is redistributed among classes at imbalance ratios of \num{1}, \num{10}, and \num{100}. Along the \emph{independent-support} axis the same nominal allocation is drawn either from the minimal patient pool the contribution caps permit or from the full eligible patient inventory, so patch counts are held exactly equal while the number of patients backing each class changes. Crossing the two yields \num{12} comparison units over three tail assignments. Nominal deprivation damaged both endpoints: the discrimination gate opened in \num{8} of its \num{9} units and the probability-quality gate in \num{8}, with case-macro balanced-accuracy deficits from \num{0.015} to \num{0.138} and tail-group likelihood deficits up to \num{1.744} nats. Independent-support deprivation, dosed at \num{0.41} to \num{0.67} nats of lost patient support at an imbalance ratio pinned to one, damaged discrimination by \num{0.026} to \num{0.030} balanced accuracy and opened the gate in all three of its units. Recovery separated sharply and asymmetrically. Where nominal allocation caused the damage, the prevalence- and nominal-support-weighted members of the confirmatory family returned between \num{0.43} and \num{0.89} of the discrimination deficit and between \num{0.54} and \num{1.23} of the probability-quality deficit. Where independent support caused it, no member of the confirmatory family and no member of the wider roster recovered anything distinguishable from zero, and the direct contrast between the signal-matched and unmatched arms was \num{0.0006} balanced accuracy. The benchmark therefore exhibits, for the first time under this protocol, a gate-passing regime that every tested mechanism fails to repair. The closing discussion converts these outcomes into design requirements and marks the conclusions that a single dataset cannot support.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
This report presents the results of the controlled patch-classification benchmark whose design, endpoints, gating rule, and inference procedure are fixed in the companion protocol \citep{queisler2026benchmarkProtocol}. Nothing in the analysis reported here was chosen after seeing an outcome: the estimands, the deficit thresholds, the confirmatory method family, the multiplicity correction, and the exploratory status of the association models were all locked before the first definitive model fit. The reporting order below follows that locked plan rather than the order in which results proved interesting, and any departure from the plan is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:realized}.

The benchmark exists to inform the design of a new mitigation method, not to rank the existing ones. Its premise is that an underserved class may lack nominal patch support, independent evidence from patients or slides, learnable signal, or representational diversity, and that these shortages are not interchangeable. Existing methods are therefore treated as instruments: each reads one signal, and the contrast among them measures whether reading the right signal matters. The report is consequently read forward into Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, where the outcomes are converted into requirements a method would have to satisfy. Dataset provenance is documented separately \citep{queisler2026datasets}, as is the signal taxonomy that locates each method within it \citep{queisler2026imbalanceMethods}.

The protocol poses three questions, restated here so that the results can be read without the companion document at hand:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] \textbf{Which shortage does controlled allocation create, and what does it damage?} Under otherwise fixed conditions, how do controlled changes in training allocation affect tail-class discrimination and calibration, and which signal profile does each condition realize?
  \item[RQ2.] \textbf{Does signal-matched mitigation recover more of the damage?} Which methods recover performance lost specifically to training imbalance, and does recovery improve when a method reads the shortage identified in RQ1?
  \item[RQ3.] \textbf{Which realized signal shortages explain variation in damage and mitigation headroom?} Across controlled conditions, are allocation severity, independent-evidence shortage, support--difficulty alignment, and diversity shortage associated with damage magnitude and subsequent recovery?
\end{description}
\noindent Sections~\ref{sec:rq1} and~\ref{sec:rq2} answer the first two. Section~\ref{sec:rq3} specifies the association models that answer the third and states why they are not fitted here.

\subsection{Scope of this report}
\label{sec:intro-scope}
The evidence below comes from one dataset. BRACS is the benchmark's most nominal-sensitive cell and the only one whose probability-quality axis has ever been damaged materially, so it is the dataset on which both gates are simultaneously available and on which a mitigation method has the most to repair. At seven classes it is also a genuine multiclass long-tailed task rather than a binary one, which is the regime the surrounding thesis addresses.

Three consequences follow and are held to throughout. First, RQ3's association models carry a random intercept over dataset--target groups, and a scale parameter over one group is not identified; the models are therefore specified in Section~\ref{sec:rq3} and fitted once a second multiclass dataset is available. Second, no claim here is a claim about generalization across datasets, including the calibration of the two deficit thresholds, which are derived from this dataset's own confirmation-seed dispersion. Third, comparisons that would require a task with few classes --- in particular whether damage grows as a fixed budget is split among fewer classes --- lie outside the scope of the benchmark as it is now planned, because the binary cells are not part of the intended programme. Section~\ref{sec:limits} restates these boundaries as scope conditions.

Two conventions govern how the remaining evidence is labelled. A result is \emph{confirmatory} only if it belongs to the five-member method family, arises from a condition that passed the relevant gate, and survives the Holm adjustment applied jointly within the dataset. Every other quantity is \emph{exploratory} or \emph{descriptive}, and is marked as such in the text and in the table caption rather than only in the surrounding prose.

\section{Allocation-Induced Damage}
\label{sec:rq1}
The controlled conditions must first be shown to have executed as specified, because the inference machinery is conditional on that. Four properties matter before any endpoint is read: whether constrained allocation reached its target severities within the prespecified tolerance bands, whether the spread arm realized a measurable independent-support shortage without disturbing the nominal allocation, whether the label-only bootstrap preflight left any class designated descriptive-only, and whether every confirmation seed block and every expected comparison is present across all three partitions. A descriptive-only designation forces both gates closed, which removes the affected evidence from the confirmatory results entirely; an incomplete comparison halts aggregation rather than being averaged over whatever is available. Appendix~\ref{app:realized} carries the full record together with the tuning selections actually used and any deviation from the locked protocol.

All four properties hold. Every moderate condition realized $\rho_{\mathrm{achieved}}$ within $[9,11]$ and every severe condition within $[90,110]$, at $\rho_{\mathrm{achieved}}=\num{10.00}$ and \num{100.38} respectively, and no condition collapsed to the balanced allocation. The spread arm holds the patch budget at exactly \num{16796} patches and the imbalance ratio at \num{1.000} while raising the contributing patient count from \num{85} to \num{103}, which is the full eligible inventory. No class failed the preflight concentration checks on the pooled evidence cell that every gated quantity uses. All three partitions completed with five confirmation seeds in every method block, the full expected set of \num{378} comparisons, and an achieved-severity spread across partitions of at most \num{0.002} in relative terms against a guard of \num{0.5}. The controlled comparison therefore rests on \num{12} comparison units: three tail assignments crossed with four deprived conditions.

\subsection{The crossed condition family}
\label{sec:rq1-crossed}
Ordinary class imbalance moves nominal patch support and independent patient support together, which is why the two cannot be separated observationally. The design separates them by holding one fixed while moving the other, using five controlled conditions and two references:
\begin{description}
  \item[Nominal axis.] The \emph{moderate} and \emph{severe} conditions redistribute the fixed patch budget at $\rho=\num{10}$ and $\rho=\num{100}$ and are read against the size-matched \emph{balanced} allocation, which draws from the same concentrated patient pool. Patient breadth is held constant; nominal counts move.
  \item[Independent-support axis.] The \emph{balanced} allocation is read against \emph{balanced (spread)}, which allocates exactly the same per-class patch counts over the full eligible patient inventory. Nominal counts are identical by construction and $\rho$ stays at one; only the number of patients backing each class moves.
  \item[Nominal axis over the broad pool.] The \emph{severe (spread)} condition applies the $\rho=\num{100}$ allocation over the spread pool and is read against \emph{balanced (spread)}. It repeats the nominal manipulation at a support breadth where independent evidence is not itself scarce.
\end{description}
\noindent The construction pushes against patient inventory rather than against the per-patient contribution caps, and that direction is what makes it feasible. A narrowing arm, which would deprive independent support by shrinking the pool, is not available on this dataset: the concentrated pool is already the smallest patient set that can supply the cross-assignment maximum patch count, so there is no slack to remove. Spreading lowers per-patient contribution instead of raising it, and is therefore feasible wherever the concentrated construction is.

Three freeze-time guards keep the axis from degenerating into a relabelling of the nominal one. A spread arm whose mean achieved log-shortage falls below \num{0.25} is refused; a spread pool that is not a strict superset of the concentrated pool is refused, so the arm adds patients rather than exchanging them; and a freeze in which the independent-support shortage never varies across comparison units is refused outright, which is the guard that detects the axis silently collapsing to a constant. The realized mean log-shortage is \num{0.350} to \num{0.577} across the three partitions, and the pools are nested by construction.

\subsection{Realized signal profiles}
\label{sec:rq1-profiles}
Allocation severity is the manipulated variable, but it is not the explanatory one. Each controlled condition is characterized instead by the signal profile it realizes: achieved imbalance ratio $\rho$ and normalized-entropy deficit $\Hnorm$, nominal patch-support shortage, independent patient-support shortage, support--difficulty alignment, and representational-diversity shortage. These quantities are measured from the frozen construction rather than inferred from the nominal target, and each is computed against the reference that isolates its own axis: the concentrated balanced allocation for the nominal conditions, and the spread balanced allocation for the balanced and severe-spread conditions.

\noindent The independent-support column is the one the crossed design was built to populate, and it varies. Its raw value is a mean log ratio of contributing patients over the classes that lost independent support, and it reaches \num{0.409}, \num{0.630}, and \num{0.673} nats on the three partitions. For scale, the full nominal step from the balanced to the moderate allocation is $\log \num{10} = \num{2.30}$ nats, so the independent axis is dosed at roughly \num{18} to \num{29}\% of the nominal axis's own smallest step. This is a deliberately modest dose, and it is the largest the patient inventory of a \num{103}-patient cohort permits once per-class contribution caps are respected. A null on this axis would consequently be weak evidence; the result reported in Section~\ref{sec:rq2} is not a null.

Two structural features of the table constrain how it should be read. The three balanced units share a single treated allocation and differ only in which assignment's spread arm serves as their reference, so they are three correlated readings of one manipulation rather than three independent replicates; their close agreement is expected and is not confirmation. Separately, units whose two largest standardized scores fall within \num{0.25} standard deviations are designated ambiguous and contribute descriptively only, never as evidence for or against signal matching. Three of the \num{12} units carry that designation, all at severe severity: native severe, reversed severe, and native severe (spread).

\subsection{Discrimination deficit and gate status}
\label{sec:rq1-discrimination}
The discrimination deficit $D_{\mathrm{BA}}$ is the case-macro balanced accuracy of the reference cross-entropy model minus that of the deprived cross-entropy model in the same unit, estimated with the patient-clustered Bayesian bootstrap and averaged with equal weight across the three partitions. The discrimination gate opens only when $D_{\mathrm{BA}}$ reaches at least \num{0.01443} and its paired \num{95}\% interval excludes zero, a threshold fixed at twice the observed confirmation-seed standard deviation so that a deficit indistinguishable from initialization noise cannot open it.

Each condition is compared against the reference that isolates its axis. Units failing the \num{0.01443} threshold or the interval condition are shown and marked as not opening the gate.}
  \label{tab:discrimination-deficit}

\noindent Eleven of the \num{12} units opened the gate; the exception is the difficulty-aligned moderate condition, whose deficit of \num{0.0150} clears the threshold but whose interval covers zero. Every one of the \num{12} deficits is positive, so neither redistributing the budget toward head classes nor concentrating it on fewer patients ever improved case-macro balanced accuracy.

Three regularities organize the table. Deficits grow with achieved severity under every assignment, most steeply under the difficulty-reversed one, which moves from \num{0.0817} at $\rho=\num{10}$ to \num{0.1323} at $\rho=\num{100}$. Deficits are largest under that same reversed assignment, which gives the least support to the classes that are easiest to separate and therefore concentrates scarcity where separability cannot compensate: at severe severity it damages roughly three times as much as the difficulty-aligned assignment. And the independent-support axis, at a fifth to a quarter of the nominal dose, produces deficits of \num{0.0264} to \num{0.0295} --- larger than the difficulty-aligned moderate condition achieves at ten times the nominal imbalance, and clear of the threshold in all three assignments.

The severe (spread) units allow the nominal manipulation to be read at a support breadth where independent evidence is plentiful. The comparison is not a like-for-like difference, since each unit is measured against its own reference, but the deficits are of the same order as their concentrated counterparts and slightly larger under the native and reversed assignments (\num{0.0847} against \num{0.0545}, and \num{0.1379} against \num{0.1323}) and slightly smaller under the aligned one (\num{0.0404} against \num{0.0430}). Nominal deprivation therefore does not depend on the pool being narrow, and is not a repackaged independent-support effect.

\subsection{Probability-quality deficit}
\label{sec:rq1-calibration}
Discrimination and probability quality can be damaged independently, so the calibration axis carries its own deficit and its own gate. The co-primary endpoint is tail-group macro negative log likelihood in nats, computed over the classes the condition places in its tail tier; its gate opens at a deficit of at least \num{0.14782} nats with a paired interval excluding zero. Expected calibration error, multiclass Brier score, and the temperature-scaled variant of each are secondary and never gate.

\noindent Eight of the \num{12} units opened the calibration gate, and the four that did not divide cleanly by axis. The native moderate condition fails on precision, at \num{0.186} nats with an interval covering zero. The three balanced units --- the entire independent-support axis --- fail on magnitude, at \num{0.132} to \num{0.135} nats against the \num{0.14782}-nat threshold. That is a narrow miss, within eleven per cent of the threshold in all three assignments and with intervals that exclude zero, so the defensible reading is that the independent axis degrades tail-group probability quality by an amount this dataset's own seed dispersion cannot certify as material, not that it leaves probability quality untouched.

Among the units that did open the gate, the deficit tracks both severity and assignment adversity, running from \num{0.379} nats at difficulty-aligned moderate to \num{1.744} nats at difficulty-reversed severe. The secondary expected calibration error moves in the same direction, from \num{0.143} to \num{0.313} across those units, against \num{0.051} at the balanced allocation. The one place where the two axes diverge is the native severe (spread) unit, whose discrimination deficit is the second largest in the benchmark (\num{0.0847}) while its calibration deficit is the smallest of the gate-passing set (\num{0.201} nats): spreading the pool leaves the model less confidently wrong about its tail than the concentrated severe allocation does, while costing more accuracy.

\subsection{Where the damage falls}
\label{sec:rq1-incidence}
Aggregate deficits do not say which classes absorbed the loss, and a mitigation method has to be told that. Recall, negative log likelihood, and Brier score are therefore also read per support tier, with the head tier the first $\lceil K/3 \rceil$ classes in the assignment's ordering and the tail tier the last $\lceil K/3 \rceil$; the per-class breakdown appears in Appendix~\ref{app:endpoints}. One reading caution applies to the spread reference: its allocation is uniform, so its tier labels carry the assignment's class ordering rather than any difference in support, and the spread of recall across its tiers measures intrinsic class difficulty rather than deprivation. It is the appropriate baseline for exactly that reason.

\noindent Under the difficulty-aligned assignment the damage lands where support rank predicts. Tail recall falls from \num{0.408} under the uniform spread allocation to \num{0.183} at moderate and \num{0.118} at severe severity, while head recall rises from \num{0.668} to \num{0.747}: the budget moves to the head and the head takes it. Under the difficulty-reversed assignment the pattern differs, and it is the body tier rather than the tail that collapses --- to \num{0.153} at severe severity, against a tail of \num{0.355} and a head of \num{0.485}. The uniform reference shows why: the body tier is already the weakest there, at \num{0.310} against a tail of \num{0.669}, because the reversed ordering places the intrinsically hardest classes in the head and the easiest in the tail, leaving the middle ranks with neither support nor separability. The allocation deepens a weakness it did not create, driving body recall to \num{0.133} under severe (spread). A mitigation method keyed to support rank alone would target the wrong tier in exactly this case.

Its purpose is to separate two readings of the same aggregate deficit: a loss that varies smoothly with support implies that support is the binding constraint, whereas classes falling well below the support trend point to a shortage that allocation alone does not describe.

\subsection{Gate routing and the natural anchor}
\label{sec:rq1-routing}
The two gates partition the comparison units into four states: discrimination only, probability quality only, both, and neither. This partition fixes the scope of Section~\ref{sec:rq2}, because recovery is expressed as a fraction of a deficit and is undefined where no deficit was established. Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing} states the routing compactly and is the table to consult before reading any recovery estimate.

\noindent Every unit is routed somewhere, and three of the four states are occupied. Seven units opened both gates: the reversed moderate condition and all six severe and severe-spread conditions. The natural condition sits outside this partition. It trains on all \num{217399} eligible training patches at the dataset's own prevalence and is therefore neither size-matched nor part of any estimand, but it is reported because it locates the controlled conditions against the imbalance the dataset presents without intervention, which is the regime a deployed method would meet.

The comparison also frames the size of the independent-support effect: redistributing the same \num{16796} patches from \num{85} patients to \num{103} buys more balanced accuracy than adding two hundred thousand patches at the natural prevalence.

\section{Mitigation Recovery}
\label{sec:rq2}
Everything in this section is restricted to the comparison units whose gates opened in Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing}. Recovery is defined as $R_M = [M(\text{method}) - M(\text{deprived CE})] / D_M$, the fraction of the established deficit that a method returns, so a unit with no established deficit has no defined recovery and is not reported here at any strength of evidence.

\subsection{The confirmatory five-family}
\label{sec:rq2-family}
The confirmatory family contains one method per signal, and all five apply a mean-one class weight to unmodified cross-entropy: prevalence-weighted cross-entropy, class-balanced cross-entropy, independent-support weighted cross-entropy, pilot-difficulty weighted cross-entropy, and semantic-scale weighted cross-entropy. Holding the intervention mechanism fixed in this way is what makes the comparison a contrast between signals rather than between loss surgeries of differing aggressiveness. Each method is tested against deprived cross-entropy by a paired partition-block permutation test. The Holm adjustment is applied jointly over the dataset's confirmatory family, which holds \num{138} members --- five methods in each of the \num{12} units on each of the two axes, plus a matched contrast for each non-ambiguous unit and axis --- of which \num{108} sit on a gate-passing axis and are therefore testable.

\noindent The family separates into the same two groups in every unit where anything recovers at all. Prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy return between \num{0.426} and \num{0.892} of the discrimination deficit across the eight nominal-axis units, and ten of their sixteen tests survive Holm adjustment. The independent-support, difficulty, and diversity members return between $-\num{0.149}$ and \num{0.197} on those same units, and not one of their \num{24} tests survives. The closest case is independent-support weighting under the reversed severe condition, at \num{0.111} with an interval of $[\num{0.046}, \num{0.184}]$ and an adjusted $p$ of \num{0.0505}; on the prespecified rule it does not survive, and it is the only cell in which that method's interval excludes zero on the discrimination axis.

The independent-support axis is where the family behaves differently, and the difference is this report's central negative result. In all three balanced units every one of the five members recovers essentially nothing: point estimates from $-\num{0.064}$ to \num{0.024}, every interval covering zero, and every adjusted $p$ equal to \num{1}. The largest point estimate belongs to semantic-scale weighting, at \num{0.021} to \num{0.024}; independent-support weighted cross-entropy, the member built to read exactly this shortage, returns \num{0.013} to \num{0.015}. A gate opened on a deficit of roughly three balanced-accuracy points, and the confirmatory family left all of it standing.

Two features of the nominal-axis results qualify the picture there. Holm adjustment bites hardest where recovery intervals are wide: the native severe and difficulty-aligned severe conditions carry prevalence-weighted point estimates of \num{0.768} and \num{0.654} and still fail adjustment, at $p=\num{0.121}$ and $p=\num{0.334}$. And the two prevalence-corner members do not separate here the way they can when an effective-number transform saturates; class-balanced cross-entropy leads in three units and prevalence weighting in five, with differences typically inside their intervals.

\subsection{Matched against unmatched signals}
\label{sec:rq2-matched}
The claim RQ2 actually tests is not that some method recovers damage but that the method reading the shortage a condition presents recovers more of it than a method reading a different shortage. Each gate-passing unit is therefore split into a matched arm, containing the family member whose signal corresponds to the dominant shortage from Table~\ref{tab:signal-profiles}, and an unmatched arm containing the remainder. Ambiguous units contribute descriptively and carry no confirmatory weight.

\noindent For the first time under this protocol the matched arm can be occupied by independent-support weighting on a unit that genuinely lacks independent support, and the contrast there is flat. All three balanced units return \num{0.0006} balanced accuracy with an interval of $[-\num{0.0013}, \num{0.0022}]$ and an adjusted $p$ of \num{1}. The matching hypothesis is not merely unsupported on this axis; the two arms are indistinguishable, because neither contains a method that moves the endpoint.

Elsewhere the contrast is signed by which shortage the unit's profile makes dominant, and the sign is predicted entirely by whether that shortage is nominal support. In the two units whose dominant shortage is nominal support the contrast is positive and survives adjustment: \num{0.0234} at native moderate and \num{0.0708} at reversed moderate. In every unit whose dominant shortage is difficulty or diversity the contrast is negative, reaching $-\num{0.0529}$ at reversed severe (spread) with an adjusted $p$ of \num{0.0006}, because the matched arm there holds exactly one member of the group that recovers nothing while the unmatched arm retains both members that work. \subsection{The wider method roster}
\label{sec:rq2-roster}
The remaining roster methods change the training objective, the sampling distribution, the decision threshold, or the classifier head, and so cannot be compared to the five-family at a fixed intervention mechanism. They are reported with intervals but without confirmatory status: balanced sampling, train-time and post-hoc logit adjustment, classifier retraining, focal loss, class-balanced focal loss, the soft-$F_1$ and soft-MCC hybrids, and set learning. Their role is to bound what is achievable on these conditions by any tested means, which is what Section~\ref{sec:discussion} needs in order to distinguish a signal that no method exploits from a deficit that no mechanism repairs.

Four of these members must be read as one mechanism rather than four. Balanced sampling, the soft-$F_1$ hybrid, the soft-MCC hybrid, and the second stage of classifier retraining all resample the training distribution toward uniformity, the last three at a strength that is fixed rather than tuned. \section{Explaining Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:rq3}
Damage magnitude and recovery headroom vary across conditions, and RQ3 asks which realized shortages track that variation. Two association models are specified, one for signed balanced-accuracy damage over the cross-entropy cells and one for recovery over the gate-passing method cells. Both use the same linear predictor over standardized quantities,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_j = \alpha + u_{g[j]} + \beta_{\rho}\log \rho_j + \beta_{\mathrm{ind}} S_{\mathrm{ind},j} + \beta_{A} A_j + \beta_{\mathrm{div}} S_{\mathrm{div},j},
  \label{eq:rq3-model}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\rho_j$ is achieved severity, $S_{\mathrm{ind},j}$ the independent-support shortage, $A_j$ the support--difficulty alignment, $S_{\mathrm{div},j}$ the diversity shortage, and $u_{g[j]}$ a random intercept over dataset--target groups that absorbs level differences between datasets. Observation errors come from the bootstrap distributions of the corresponding endpoints.

These models are not fitted in this report, and the reason is structural rather than practical. The quantity that makes Equation~\eqref{eq:rq3-model} worth fitting is $\sigma_u$, the scale of the dataset--target intercepts, which answers whether a shortage-to-damage association is a property of the mechanism or of the dataset. A scale parameter over a single group is not identified: the per-partition fits computed here return $\sigma_u=\num{0.368}$ on every one, which is the prior scale rather than an estimate, and the leave-one-group-out diagnostic that the protocol specifies as the models' only accuracy check has nothing to hold out. Reporting coefficients under those conditions would present a prior as a finding.

The per-partition cells are computed and frozen so that the combined fit can be assembled without repeating the bootstrap. Two properties of the design bound what that fit will be able to say even once a second multiclass dataset is available. The identifiability guard excludes binary-target cells from both fits, because a support--difficulty alignment computed over two classes is always $\pm 1$ and would confound the pooled coefficient, so only multiclass datasets can contribute groups. And with two groups $\sigma_u$ is estimable but weakly, while leave-one-group-out reduces to two single-group holdouts. Any RQ3 conclusion reached at that point will be a two-group statement, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-requirements} marks the design requirement that depends on it as open.

What can be said without the models is what Section~\ref{sec:rq1-discrimination} establishes descriptively. Damage rises monotonically with achieved severity under all three assignments; it is roughly three times larger under the difficulty-reversed assignment than under the aligned one at matched severity; and the independent-support axis produces damage at a fifth to a quarter of the nominal dose, which is the single observation in this report that a nominal-only account of shortage cannot absorb.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The preceding sections establish what the controlled allocations damaged and how much of that damage the tested methods returned. This section reads those outcomes as design evidence. The question it answers is not which existing method won, but what a mitigation method would have to read, and to do, in order to address the shortages this dataset actually presents.

\subsection{What the evidence supports}
\label{sec:discussion-evidence}
On RQ1, both manipulated axes damage the endpoints, and they damage them differently. Nominal deprivation opened the discrimination gate in eight of its nine units and the probability-quality gate in eight, with damage growing in severity and in assignment adversity, and landing on the body tier rather than the tail wherever the assignment gives the easiest classes the least support. Independent-support deprivation, at a fifth to a quarter of the nominal dose and with patch counts held exactly equal, opened the discrimination gate in all three of its units at deficits of \num{0.026} to \num{0.030}, and fell just under the probability-quality threshold at \num{0.132} to \num{0.135} nats. The two axes are therefore not redundant: a class can be measurably underserved with no shortfall in its patch count at all.

On RQ2, recovery separates the confirmatory family into two groups along the same split in every unit where anything recovers, and the split is by signal, not by intervention strength. Prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy return most of both deficits on the nominal axis; independent-support, difficulty, and diversity weighting return essentially none of the discrimination deficit and degrade tail-group probability quality in all \num{24} of their calibration cells, every one surviving multiplicity adjustment. The matched-$\beta$ diagnostic excludes tuned weight strength as the explanation. On the independent-support axis nothing recovers: not the matched member, not the prevalence corner, and not one of the nine exploratory mechanisms, and the direct matched-versus-unmatched contrast is \num{0.0006} balanced accuracy with an interval covering zero.

On RQ3 the report makes no claim. The models are specified and their cells are frozen; the fit requires a second multiclass dataset--target group before its variance components mean anything.

\subsection{Which shortage a mitigation method must read}
\label{sec:discussion-signal}
A method must estimate the shortage that binds, so the empirical question is whether the dominant shortage was identifiable and whether reading it helped. On this dataset the dominant shortage was identifiable in nine of \num{12} units, and reading it helped only where it was nominal support. That much repeats a familiar conclusion. What is new is the case in which the dominant shortage is independent support and reading it still does not help.

That case is a real measurement rather than a structural artefact. In a design where a single per-class evidence pool serves every condition, independent support cannot vary, and a method built to read it is being tested on conditions in which the quantity it reads is constant --- a null under those circumstances says nothing. The spread arm removes that objection: independent support varies by \num{0.41} to \num{0.67} nats between the two arms, the pools are nested so the manipulation adds patients rather than exchanging them, and the resulting deficit is large enough to open a prespecified gate. Independent-support weighted cross-entropy then recovers \num{0.013} to \num{0.015} of it. The inference the evidence supports is that a mean-one class weight computed from patient counts does not repair damage caused by a shortage of patients, at this dose, on this dataset.

The mechanism that failure suggests is worth stating because it constrains method design directly. All five confirmatory members apply a per-class scalar to the loss, which can only change how heavily each class's examples are emphasized. When a class lacks patients rather than patches, its patches are correlated: they carry fewer independent observations of the class than their count implies, and no reweighting of that count adds information the sample does not contain. A method that addresses independent-support shortage must therefore do something other than reweight --- draw on evidence outside the class's own examples, or change what is estimated rather than how heavily it is weighted.

The frequency of the ambiguous designation qualifies the routing question separately. Three of \num{12} units, all at severe severity, have their two largest shortage scores within a quarter of a standard deviation. A taxonomy that cannot separate the top two axes in a quarter of its conditions does not partition sharply enough to route a method by selecting among signals, which points toward a method that combines them.

\subsection{Signal against mechanism}
\label{sec:discussion-mechanism}
Because the five confirmatory methods share one intervention mechanism and differ only in the signal their weights read, the spread of recovery across them isolates how much leverage the choice of signal carries. That spread is wide on the nominal axis, running from below zero to \num{0.892} within single units, so signal choice determines whether a weighted-loss method works at all there. The spread is not, however, evidence that a better shortage estimate would help, because it is bimodal rather than graded: two members work almost everywhere on that axis and three work almost nowhere, with little in between.

The independent-support axis separates signal from mechanism more cleanly than the nominal axis can, because there the mechanism is held fixed across the five members and the wider roster fails alongside them. Nine exploratory methods spanning resampling, logit adjustment, decision-threshold correction, classifier retraining, and set learning were applied to a deficit of roughly three balanced-accuracy points, and none returned a positive fraction of it whose interval excludes zero. When both the signal contrast and the mechanism contrast come back null on the same units, the limitation lies not in the choice among tested designs but in what all of them share.

The roster's harmful members sharpen that reading. Classifier retraining and set learning are the two mechanisms that most aggressively rebalance the effective training distribution, and they are also the two that damage the independent-support units most, at $-\num{0.73}$ to $-\num{1.05}$. Rebalancing toward classes whose examples come from few patients amplifies those patients' idiosyncrasies rather than the class's signal, which is the failure mode a patient-aware method would have to avoid by construction.

\subsection{Where recovery ceilings}
\label{sec:discussion-ceiling}
The units that define an unmet need are those in which a gate opened and no method, confirmatory or exploratory, returned a substantial fraction of the deficit. On this dataset there are four of them, out of eleven discrimination-gate units. Three are the independent-support units, where nothing recovers at all. The fourth is difficulty-aligned severe (spread), where the best tested mechanism of any kind reaches \num{0.57}. A fifth case sits on the other axis: the native severe (spread) unit, where both prevalence-corner members return negative probability-quality recovery even though both repair most of the same unit's discrimination deficit.

This is the finding that most changes what a new method would be for. The ceiling on the nominal axis is set by neither the data nor the frozen representation --- existing mechanisms reach most of that damage, and a new method cannot be motivated there by damage left unrepaired. The ceiling on the independent-support axis is set by the mechanism the whole roster shares. A method motivated by this benchmark is therefore a method for the second regime, and its claim would be a claim about patient-level evidence rather than about class counts.

The complementary case remains part of the specification. The difficulty-aligned moderate condition damaged probability quality without a certifiable discrimination deficit, and the three independent-support units damaged discrimination without a certifiable probability-quality deficit. A method that intervenes on the wrong axis spends capacity and risks degrading an endpoint that was not damaged, and Section~\ref{sec:rq2-calibration} shows that risk is real: in all \num{24} cells where a non-prevalence signal was applied to a calibration-damaged unit, tail-group probability quality fell below the untreated reference. Knowing when, and on which axis, not to act is part of the specification rather than an afterthought.

\subsection{Design requirements and open questions}
\label{sec:discussion-requirements}
The preceding subsections resolve into a set of requirements for a mitigation method, each traceable to a specific result, annotated with the strength of evidence behind it and, where relevant, with what a second dataset would have to confirm.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Where the shortage is nominal, the method must read prevalence or nominal support, and must not rely on a difficulty or diversity estimate as its primary signal. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the five-family separation in Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory} across the eight nominal-axis discrimination units.
  \item It must not degrade tail-group probability quality while repairing discrimination. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the \num{24} negative calibration-recovery cells in Table~\ref{tab:calibration-recovery}, all of which survive Holm adjustment.
  \item It must decline to intervene where allocation produced no material deficit on the axis it targets, which on this dataset is the difficulty-aligned moderate condition on discrimination and the three independent-support units on probability quality. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the gate routing in Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing}.
  \item Independent-support shortage requires a mechanism other than per-class loss reweighting. \emph{Confirmatory within this dataset}, from the joint null of the confirmatory family and the exploratory roster on the three balanced units. \emph{Open across datasets}: this cohort carries the weakest independent dose the candidate datasets offer, so a replication at a stronger dose is needed before the null is read as a property of reweighting rather than of this dose.
  \item It must not require access to test prevalence, labelled data beyond the training partition, or per-condition tuning that a deployment could not perform. \emph{Design constraint}, carried from the protocol rather than established by a result here.
  \item Whether the shortage estimate must be formed per condition rather than transferred across datasets. \emph{Open}: this is the requirement RQ3's variance components were specified to settle, and it needs at least a second multiclass dataset--target group before Equation~\eqref{eq:rq3-model} can be fitted.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent Requirements four and six are the two that a second dataset changes, and they are the two on which a method design should not be committed yet.

\subsection{What a single dataset cannot settle}
\label{sec:discussion-open}
Four questions are left open deliberately, and are listed here so that they are not inferred from silence.

The first is whether the independent-support null generalizes. The independent dose realized here, \num{0.41} to \num{0.67} nats, is the weakest of the four datasets on which the crossed construction was measured; a multiclass cohort with a larger patient inventory reaches \num{1.46} to \num{1.79} nats, close to the nominal step itself. If reweighting fails there too, the negative result becomes a statement about the mechanism. If it succeeds there, the result becomes a statement about dose, and requirement four above is withdrawn.

The second is RQ3 in full, as set out in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}. The third is the external validity of the two deficit thresholds, which are derived from this dataset's own confirmation-seed dispersion and therefore calibrate materiality to it; whether they are comparable in scale to thresholds derived the same way elsewhere is unverified, and every gate decision in this report inherits that limit.

The fourth is not deferred but excluded. Whether damage grows as a fixed budget is split among fewer classes is a question about binary and low-cardinality targets, and the binary cells lie outside the scope of this programme. It is recorded here as a boundary of the design rather than as pending work.

\section{Conclusion and Scope}
\label{sec:limits}
This report answers two of the protocol's three questions on one dataset. Controlled allocation damages tail-class discrimination and probability quality along two axes that ordinary imbalance confounds, and the crossed construction separates them: nominal patch deprivation and independent patient deprivation each open a prespecified gate, at doses that differ by a factor of four to five. Signal-matched mitigation recovers most of the nominal damage through prevalence and nominal-support weighting alone, and recovers none of the independent-support damage through any tested signal or any tested mechanism. The contribution is the design evidence assembled in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, which states what a mitigation method must read, what it must not degrade, where it must decline to act, and --- for the first time under this protocol --- a regime in which the existing roster leaves the damage standing.

Five scope conditions bound every claim above. The evidence comes from one dataset, so no statement here is a statement about transfer, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-open} lists the questions a second multiclass dataset is required to close. The three partitions are locked design repetitions of that dataset, not independent cohorts, so the intervals quantify variation across partitionings and initializations rather than across populations. The three independent-support units share a single treated allocation and differ only in their reference arm, so they are correlated readings rather than three replicates. The benchmark covers patch classification only, where each prediction unit carries its own label; at whole-slide level, reallocating labelled slides changes nominal and independent support together, so the two axes could not be separated in the way this design requires. And temperature scaling is reported as a diagnostic throughout and never gates a decision.

\noindent None of the \num{196} selections terminated at a learning-rate envelope boundary, so in no case did the envelope rather than the validation data determine the choice. Three properties of the selection procedure are stated here as limits on the claims that rest on it rather than as defects.

The selection objective is natural-prevalence validation balanced accuracy, tie-broken by macro $F_1$ and then by negative log likelihood. Tail-group negative log likelihood is a co-primary endpoint with its own gate, and no method was ever tuned for it. Every statement in Section~\ref{sec:rq2-calibration} about a method degrading probability quality is therefore a statement about a method optimized for a different endpoint.

Selection also pools across tail assignments: one configuration is chosen per condition and method and applied to all three assignments, which the table records as repeated rows, and all \num{60} condition--method cells that span more than one assignment carry identical selections. Since the assignment determines which classes need help, this pooling systematically dilutes the difficulty- and diversity-reading methods relative to a per-assignment selection, and their null results should be read with that in mind.

The tuning budget itself differs by method: \num{16} candidates for most, four for cross-entropy and classifier retraining, and a single shard for post-hoc logit adjustment, which requires no training of its own.

Three further properties of the selections change what a method label denotes and are recorded for that reason. The search selects the zero-strength point in \num{29} of the \num{196} selections: \num{13} for the soft-$F_1$ hybrid, nine for the soft-MCC hybrid, and seven for focal loss. At zero strength the soft hybrids reduce to balanced-sampling cross-entropy, because their auxiliary term vanishes while their forced balanced sampler does not, and focal loss reduces to class-weighted cross-entropy, because its class-aware $\alpha$ term applies at unit strength independently of $\gamma$. Where those selections were made, the roster row reports the reduced method rather than the named one.

One consequence of that reduction is a defect in the search rather than in the fitted models, and it is recorded rather than repaired because repairing it would change the tuning protocol between this dataset and any replication. The search treats focal loss as anchored to plain cross-entropy, so its $\gamma = 0$ candidate is scored using cross-entropy's already-computed validation metrics instead of being trained. Because $\alpha$ applies unconditionally, that candidate is in fact class-weighted cross-entropy and not plain cross-entropy, so the seven selections that landed on $\gamma = 0$ were made against the wrong metrics. The confirmation runs train the correct objective, and focal loss is an exploratory roster member that enters no confirmatory test, so no gated or Holm-adjusted quantity in this report depends on it; its recovery estimates in Table~\ref{tab:roster-recovery} should nonetheless be read as estimates of a configuration that was not selected on its own evidence.

Two deviations from the locked protocol were made before any definitive gate row was read. The two deficit-gate thresholds were derived rather than assumed: the protocol had fixed $D_{\mathrm{BA}} \ge \num{0.02}$ and $D_{\mathrm{cal}} \ge \num{0.05}$ nats without a stated derivation, and the discrimination value conflicted with the support pilot's own materiality constant of \num{0.01} for the same endpoint under the same kind of manipulation. Both were replaced by quantities the protocol already fixes, $\max(\num{0.01}, 2\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}})=\num{0.01443}$ for discrimination and $2\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}}=\num{0.14782}$ nats for calibration, where $\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}}$ is the confirmation-seed standard deviation of the corresponding balanced-condition endpoint on this dataset. Separately, the gate-defining estimator is case-macro rather than patch-micro, and the evidence cell is pooled across a case's partition appearances rather than taken per partition; both correct a mismatch between a diagnostic and the estimator it certifies, since the bootstrap draws one shared weight per case over the union of the three test frames.

\noindent The exposure budget is matched to within \num{0.6}\% across the roster, which is the property that makes the recovery comparison a comparison of methods rather than of data volume. Every method processes the same \num{503880} examples as cross-entropy to within rounding, except set learning, which exceeds it by \num{2727} to \num{2983} examples because its per-update example count depends on a tuned set size and the derived update count must be integral, and classifier retraining, which exceeds it by \num{129}. Post-hoc logit adjustment trains nothing of its own and reuses the cross-entropy checkpoint, so its processed-example difference is the full budget with a negative sign; its cost is a single pass to fit one scalar.

That matched exposure is achieved through unequal update counts, which is the intended trade. Set learning takes roughly \num{3800} fewer updates than cross-entropy at \num{641} more examples per update, and classifier retraining splits its budget between two stages in a fixed ratio. Accelerator time, peak memory, and parameter counts differ by amounts their intervals absorb in every case, so no roster method's recovery on this dataset is bought with materially more computation than the reference.
