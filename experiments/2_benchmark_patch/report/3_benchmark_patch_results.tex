\begin{abstract}
\begingroup
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}
\noindent\textbf{Status.} This is a legacy update-matched execution; its recovery and cost conclusions are descriptive pending the exposure-matched rerun.\par
\noindent A class can be underserved for several distinct reasons, and the count that marks it as rare does not say which. This report presents the outcomes of the controlled patch-classification benchmark specified in the companion protocol \citep{queisler2026benchmarkProtocol}, which manipulates only the allocation of a fixed training budget among classes and measures what that manipulation damages. CAMELYON16, TCGA-UT, BRACS, and PANDA are evaluated with frozen Virchow2 features under size-matched balanced, moderate, and severe allocations, against natural-prevalence validation and test cohorts held constant across conditions. Three questions are answered in order: which shortage the controlled allocation realizes and what it damages, whether a mitigation method that reads the shortage matching that profile recovers more of the damage than one reading a different signal, and which realized shortages explain variation in damage magnitude and recovery headroom. Mitigation recovery is interpreted only for conditions whose allocation-induced deficit passes the prespecified gate, and the confirmatory family holds the intervention mechanism fixed so that the signal a method reads is the only quantity contrasted. Controlled allocation opened the discrimination gate in \num{12} of \num{18} comparison units and the probability-quality gate in \num{6}, all of them on BRACS, with balanced-accuracy deficits spanning \num{0.0007} to \num{0.134}; damage grew with achieved severity and with the number of classes among which the fixed budget was divided. Recovery separated sharply but not along the axis the design was built to test: the prevalence- and nominal-support-weighted members of the confirmatory family returned most of the deficit wherever a gate opened, while the independent-support, difficulty, and diversity members returned essentially none of it and degraded tail probability quality further. The closing discussion converts these outcomes into design requirements for a mitigation method that reads shortage rather than prevalence alone, which is the purpose the benchmark serves.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\noindent\textbf{Status.} These are legacy update-matched results. Because methods consumed unequal examples per update, recovery and cost conclusions are descriptive only until the exposure-matched rerun.

This report presents the results of the controlled patch-classification benchmark whose design, endpoints, gating rule, and inference procedure are fixed in the companion protocol \citep{queisler2026benchmarkProtocol}. Nothing in the analysis reported here was chosen after seeing an outcome: the estimands, the deficit thresholds, the confirmatory method family, the multiplicity correction, and the exploratory status of the association models were all locked before the first definitive model fit. The reporting order below follows that locked plan rather than the order in which results proved interesting, and any departure from the plan is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:realized}.

The benchmark exists to inform the design of a new mitigation method, not to rank the existing ones. Its premise is that an underserved class may lack nominal patch support, independent evidence from patients or slides, learnable signal, or representational diversity, and that these shortages are not interchangeable. Existing methods are therefore treated as instruments: each reads one signal, and the contrast among them measures whether reading the right signal matters. The report is consequently read forward into Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, where the outcomes are converted into requirements a method would have to satisfy. Dataset provenance is documented separately \citep{queisler2026datasets}, as is the signal taxonomy that locates each method within it \citep{queisler2026imbalanceMethods}.

The protocol poses three questions, restated here so that the results can be read without the companion document at hand:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] \textbf{Which shortage does controlled allocation create, and what does it damage?} Under otherwise fixed conditions, how do controlled changes in training allocation affect tail-class discrimination and calibration, and which signal profile does each condition realize?
  \item[RQ2.] \textbf{Does signal-matched mitigation recover more of the damage?} Which methods recover performance lost specifically to training imbalance, and does recovery improve when a method reads the shortage identified in RQ1?
  \item[RQ3.] \textbf{Which realized signal shortages explain variation in damage and mitigation headroom?} Across controlled conditions, are allocation severity, independent-evidence shortage, support--difficulty alignment, and diversity shortage associated with damage magnitude and subsequent recovery?
\end{description}
\noindent The three sections that follow answer them in that order. Section~\ref{sec:rq1} establishes whether a recoverable deficit exists and which signals characterize the conditions that produced it; the deficit gate defined there determines which conditions Section~\ref{sec:rq2} is permitted to interpret. Section~\ref{sec:rq3} then examines why deficit size and recovery headroom vary, as an exploratory association analysis rather than a second method ranking.

Two conventions govern how the evidence is labelled throughout. A result is \emph{confirmatory} only if it belongs to the five-member method family, arises from a condition that passed the relevant gate, and survives the Holm adjustment applied jointly within its dataset. Every other quantity is \emph{exploratory} or \emph{descriptive}, and is marked as such in the text and in the table caption rather than only in the surrounding prose.

\section{Allocation-Induced Damage}
\label{sec:rq1}
The controlled conditions must first be shown to have executed as specified, because the inference machinery is conditional on that. Three properties matter before any endpoint is read: whether constrained allocation reached its target severities within the prespecified tolerance bands, whether the label-only bootstrap preflight left any dataset designated descriptive-only, and whether every confirmation seed block and every expected comparison is present across all three partitions. A descriptive-only designation forces both gates closed for that dataset, which removes it from the confirmatory results entirely; an incomplete comparison halts aggregation rather than being averaged over whatever is available. Appendix~\ref{app:realized} carries the full record together with the tuning selections actually used and any deviation from the locked protocol.

All three properties hold. Every moderate condition realized $\rho_{\mathrm{achieved}}$ within $[9,11]$ and every severe condition within $[90,110]$, the tightest case being the difficulty-aligned severe condition on TCGA-UT at $\rho_{\mathrm{achieved}}=\num{90.11}$ to \num{91.02}; no condition collapsed to the balanced allocation, so none is degenerate. No class on any dataset failed the preflight concentration checks, so no dataset is descriptive-only and both gates remain available everywhere. All four datasets carry three completed partitions, five confirmation seeds in every method block, the full expected set of comparisons on each partition, and an achieved-severity spread across partitions of at most \num{1.0}\% of the smallest realized value. The controlled comparison therefore rests on \num{18} comparison units: six on BRACS and six on TCGA-UT, which retain three tail assignments each, four on CAMELYON16, and two on PANDA, whose binary target admits only the native assignment once duplicated mappings are omitted.

\subsection{Realized signal profiles}
\label{sec:rq1-profiles}
Allocation severity is the manipulated variable, but it is not the explanatory one. Each controlled condition is characterized instead by the signal profile it realizes: achieved imbalance ratio $\rho$ and normalized-entropy deficit $\Hnorm$, nominal patch-support shortage, independent patient or slide support shortage, support--difficulty alignment, and representational-diversity shortage. These quantities are measured from the frozen construction rather than inferred from the nominal target, so a condition built to a severe target may still present a mild independent-support shortage, or the reverse.

Table~\ref{tab:signal-profiles} reports the standardized shortage scores per comparison unit together with the dominant shortage each unit presents. The dominant shortage is what makes the matched and unmatched arms of Section~\ref{sec:rq2} interpretable, so units whose two largest scores fall within \num{0.25} standard deviations are designated ambiguous and are reported descriptively only, never as evidence for or against signal matching. Six of the \num{18} units carry that designation: BRACS under the native moderate and difficulty-aligned severe conditions, CAMELYON16 under difficulty-reversed moderate, PANDA under native moderate, and TCGA-UT under native moderate and difficulty-aligned severe.

\noindent One column of that table requires comment before any of it is interpreted, because it constrains what the rest of the report can conclude. The standardized independent-support shortage is exactly zero in every one of the \num{18} units. This is a consequence of the design rather than a property of the datasets: the protocol fixes one per-class pool of partitioning units and slides and makes the same pool available to the balanced condition and to every imbalanced condition, so reallocating patches among classes changes how many patches a class draws but not the independent evidence it may draw from. Three consequences follow and are carried through the remainder of the report. Independent-support weighted cross-entropy can never be matched to a shortage that the design actually created. A unit whose four shortage scores are all negative will be labelled with independent support as its dominant shortage purely because zero is then the largest of them, which is what happens in the difficulty-reversed moderate conditions on BRACS and TCGA-UT. And the independent-support coefficient of the RQ3 models in Section~\ref{sec:rq3} is fixed at zero by a predictor with no variance, not estimated from data. The benchmark manipulates allocation within a fixed pool of patients and slides by construction, and the price of that control is that one of the four taxonomy axes is held constant rather than varied.

\subsection{Discrimination deficit and gate status}
\label{sec:rq1-discrimination}
The discrimination deficit $D_{\mathrm{BA}}$ is the case-macro balanced accuracy of the size-matched balanced cross-entropy reference minus that of the imbalanced cross-entropy model in the same condition, estimated with the patient-clustered Bayesian bootstrap and averaged with equal weight across the three partitions. The discrimination gate opens for a comparison unit only when $D_{\mathrm{BA}}$ reaches at least \num{0.01443} and its paired \num{95}\% interval excludes zero, a threshold fixed at twice the observed seed-level standard deviation so that a deficit indistinguishable from initialization noise cannot open it.

Table~\ref{tab:discrimination-deficit} gives the deficit, its interval, and the gate outcome for every combination of dataset, severity, and tail assignment. Deficits are reported for all units, including those that fail the threshold, the interval condition, or both, because a condition that produced no measurable damage is itself a finding about when mitigation is warranted.

  \label{tab:discrimination-deficit}

\noindent Twelve of the \num{18} units opened the gate. Every one of the \num{18} deficits is positive, and only two intervals cover zero, on the CAMELYON16 native moderate and TCGA-UT difficulty-aligned moderate conditions, so redistributing a fixed budget toward head classes never improved case-macro balanced accuracy. Three regularities organize the table. Deficits grow with achieved severity in every dataset and tail assignment: BRACS under the difficulty-reversed assignment moves from \num{0.081} at $\rho=10$ to \num{0.134} at $\rho=100$, and PANDA from \num{0.026} to \num{0.146}. Deficits grow with the difficulty-reversed assignment, which gives the least support to the classes that are easiest to separate and therefore concentrates scarcity where it cannot be compensated: on BRACS the reversed assignment damages roughly three times as much as the difficulty-aligned one at both severities. Finally, the two binary datasets and the two multiclass datasets do not behave alike, but not in the direction class count alone would suggest: PANDA, with two classes, records the largest severe deficit of all (\num{0.146}), while TCGA-UT, with \num{30}, records deficits of at most \num{0.016}. What separates them is how much of the fixed budget the tail retains. A binary severe allocation places \num{99}\% of the budget in one class, whereas a \num{30}-class allocation on a logarithmic support profile leaves the smallest class with a share that is small relative to the head but still absolutely large, which is also visible in the normalized-entropy deficit: \num{0.920} for PANDA severe against \num{0.179} for TCGA-UT severe at the same nominal $\rho=100$.

\subsection{Probability-quality deficit}
\label{sec:rq1-calibration}
Discrimination and probability quality can be damaged independently, so the calibration axis carries its own deficit and its own gate. The co-primary endpoint is tail-group macro negative log likelihood in nats, computed over the classes that the condition places in the tail tier; its gate opens at a deficit of at least \num{0.18237} nats with a paired interval excluding zero. Expected calibration error, multiclass Brier score, and the temperature-scaled variant of each are secondary and never gate.

Table~\ref{tab:calibration-deficit} reports the tail-group deficit and gate outcome per unit alongside crossed patient-bootstrap intervals on expected calibration error. Temperature scaling is fitted on natural-prevalence validation data and reported only as a diagnostic of how much of the observed miscalibration a single global scalar removes; Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} carries the raw and scaled comparison in full.

\noindent The calibration axis behaves very differently from the discrimination axis, and the difference is the sharpest single contrast in the report. All six BRACS units opened the calibration gate, with deficits from \num{0.281} to \num{2.047} nats, and no unit on any other dataset came close: the largest deficit outside BRACS is \num{0.144} nats, below the \num{0.18237} threshold. More striking, seven units record a \emph{negative} tail-group deficit, meaning the imbalanced model assigns better-calibrated probabilities to its own tail classes than the size-matched balanced reference does. Five of those seven are on TCGA-UT, where the effect reaches $-\num{0.201}$ nats; the other two are CAMELYON16 and PANDA under their moderate conditions. The secondary expected calibration error moves in the same direction as the primary endpoint wherever the gate opened, rising from \num{0.159} to \num{0.317} across the BRACS units as severity and assignment adversity increase, and remains at or below \num{0.026} on CAMELYON16 and PANDA throughout.

The reading these numbers support is that tail-group probability quality is damaged only where the tail classes are genuinely hard under the frozen representation. BRACS is the one dataset whose balanced-condition balanced accuracy sits near \num{0.49} on seven classes, so its tail classes are poorly separated even with even support, and removing support from them collapses the probability mass they receive. On CAMELYON16 and PANDA the tail remains highly separable, and on TCGA-UT the balanced reference already reaches \num{0.755} across \num{30} classes. Where a class is easy, concentrating the budget elsewhere costs accuracy on it without making the model confidently wrong about it, and the tail-group likelihood can even improve because a smaller tail allocation shrinks the model's tendency to over-assign probability to the tail on head-class examples.

\subsection{Where the damage falls}
\label{sec:rq1-incidence}
Aggregate deficits do not say which classes absorbed the loss, and a mitigation method has to be told that. Recall, $F_1$, negative log likelihood, and Brier score are therefore also read per class and per support tier, with the head tier the first $\lceil K/3 \rceil$ classes by allocated support and the tail tier the last $\lceil K/3 \rceil$. Table~\ref{tab:tier-endpoints} gives the tier-level endpoints under each condition; the per-class breakdown appears in Appendix~\ref{app:endpoints}. The balanced reference and the natural anchor induce no support ordering and therefore carry no tier assignment, so they do not appear there.

\noindent The tier view contradicts the simplest expectation, which is that damage lands on the tail. It does so under the difficulty-aligned assignment, where BRACS tail recall falls to \num{0.205} at moderate and \num{0.120} at severe severity against head recall above \num{0.73}. Under the difficulty-reversed assignment the pattern inverts: BRACS severe tail recall is \num{0.343} while the \emph{body} tier reaches only \num{0.130}, below both the head and the tail. The reversed assignment gives the hardest classes the most support and the easiest classes the least, so the tail keeps a recall floor supplied by separability rather than by support, and the loss concentrates in the middle ranks, which have neither enough support nor enough intrinsic separability. A mitigation method keyed to support rank alone would target the wrong tier in exactly this case.

\label{sec:rq1-routing}
The two gates partition the comparison units into four states: discrimination only, probability quality only, both, and neither. This partition is the single result that fixes the scope of Section~\ref{sec:rq2}, because recovery is expressed as a fraction of a deficit and is undefined where no deficit was established. Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing} states the routing compactly for all units and is the table to consult before reading any recovery estimate.

\section{Mitigation Recovery}
\label{sec:rq2}
Everything in this section is restricted to the comparison units whose gates opened in Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing}. Recovery is defined as $R_M = [M(\text{method}) - M(\text{imbalanced CE})] / D_M$, the fraction of the established deficit that a method returns, so a unit with no established deficit has no defined recovery and is not reported here at any strength of evidence. Units designated descriptive-only by the bootstrap preflight are excluded on the same grounds; no unit was excluded on that ground.

\subsection{The confirmatory five-family}
\label{sec:rq2-family}
The confirmatory family contains one method per signal, and all five apply a mean-one class weight to unmodified cross-entropy: prevalence-weighted cross-entropy, class-balanced cross-entropy, independent-support weighted cross-entropy, pilot-difficulty weighted cross-entropy, and semantic-scale weighted cross-entropy. Holding the intervention mechanism fixed in this way is what makes the comparison a contrast between signals rather than between loss surgeries of differing aggressiveness. Each method is tested against imbalanced cross-entropy by a paired partitioning-unit-block permutation test, and the Holm adjustment is applied jointly within a dataset over the five method tests plus the matched contrast for each gate-passing unit.

Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory} reports recovery with its interval, the unadjusted and adjusted $p$-values, and the resulting status for each unit and method.

\noindent The family separates into two groups, and the separation is the same in all \num{12} gate-passing units. Prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy recover most of the deficit nearly everywhere: on BRACS they return between \num{0.743} and \num{0.997} of it, on CAMELYON16 between \num{0.822} and \num{0.924}, and on TCGA-UT between \num{0.792} and \num{0.828}. The independent-support, difficulty, and diversity members recover essentially nothing on BRACS and TCGA-UT, with point estimates between $-\num{0.099}$ and \num{0.370} across those \num{24} cells and intervals covering zero in almost every one of them. The one dataset where the non-prevalence signals do move an endpoint is CAMELYON16, where semantic-scale weighting recovers \num{0.655} under the native severe condition and \num{0.509} under the reversed one, and independent-support weighting recovers \num{0.480} under the reversed condition; on a two-class target, however, the diversity and independent-support weights are two-point orderings and cannot be distinguished from a coarse prevalence correction on the mechanism they apply.

PANDA is the informative exception within the working group. There prevalence weighting recovers \num{1.162} at moderate and \num{0.968} at severe severity, while class-balanced cross-entropy, which reads nominal support, recovers only \num{0.014} and \num{0.118}. The two methods differ solely in whether the weight is proportional to inverse frequency or to the effective-number reweighting of nominal counts, and at $\rho=100$ on a two-class problem the effective-number transform saturates and returns nearly uniform weights. The two members of the family that share the prevalence and nominal-support corner of the taxonomy therefore do not behave interchangeably, which matters directly for the matched contrast below.

Holm adjustment within a dataset changes the picture only at its margin, and it bites hardest where the family is largest. BRACS opened both gates in all six units, so its family holds \num{68} tests: five methods in each of the six units on each of the two axes, plus the matched contrast wherever the unit is not ambiguous. Under that correction four of the six prevalence-weighted discrimination tests survive and two do not, despite recovery point estimates above \num{0.92}, because the two moderate conditions that fail have wide recovery intervals. On CAMELYON16, PANDA, and TCGA-UT, whose families hold \num{12}, \num{11}, and \num{12} tests because only the discrimination gate opened, every prevalence-weighted discrimination test survives at adjusted $p \le \num{0.02}$.

\subsection{Matched against unmatched signals}
\label{sec:rq2-matched}
The claim RQ2 actually tests is not that some method recovers damage but that the method reading the shortage a condition presents recovers more of it than a method reading a different shortage. Each gate-passing unit is therefore split into a matched arm, containing the family member whose signal corresponds to the dominant shortage from Table~\ref{tab:signal-profiles}, and an unmatched arm containing the remainder. Ambiguous units, where the two largest shortage scores fall within \num{0.25} standard deviations, contribute descriptively and carry no confirmatory weight.

Table~\ref{tab:matched-contrast} gives the direct matched-versus-unmatched contrast per unit with its interval and adjusted $p$-value, and records which family member in fact recovered most. Two further quantities qualify how that contrast should be read. The first is the matched-$\beta$ diagnostic arm, in which independent-support weighted cross-entropy is refitted at the effective weight strength selected for class-balanced cross-entropy; it separates an advantage attributable to the signal from one attributable to a more aggressive tuned intervention, and appears in Table~\ref{tab:matched-beta}. The second is agreement between the dominant shortage and the method that in fact recovered most, which is reported per unit because a systematic mismatch would indicate that the shortage taxonomy, not the methods, is what fails to describe these conditions.

\noindent The contrast is signed in opposite directions on different datasets, and the sign is fully predicted by which shortage the unit's profile makes dominant. Where the dominant shortage is nominal support, the matched arm contains prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy, and the contrast is positive: \num{0.011} and \num{0.010} on the two CAMELYON16 severe units and \num{0.084} on PANDA severe, all surviving Holm adjustment. Where the dominant shortage is difficulty, diversity, or independent support, the matched arm contains exactly one method from the group that recovers nothing while the unmatched arm retains both methods that work, and the contrast is negative in every such unit: from $-\num{0.006}$ to $-\num{0.057}$ on discrimination, across four BRACS and two TCGA-UT units, and from $-\num{0.430}$ to $-\num{2.695}$ nats on probability quality.

The agreement column makes the same point without the arithmetic of the contrast. In all \num{18} gate-passing rows the family member that recovered most is either prevalence-weighted or class-balanced cross-entropy. The dominant shortage and the best recoverer therefore agree exactly when, and only when, the dominant shortage is nominal support. This is not evidence that signal matching works; it is evidence that on these datasets only one corner of the taxonomy is served by a method that recovers anything, so the matched contrast measures whether the unit happened to land in that corner.

The matched-$\beta$ diagnostic rules out the most obvious alternative explanation. If independent-support weighting failed because its tuned weight strength was too timid rather than because its signal is uninformative, refitting it at the strength selected for class-balanced cross-entropy should close part of the gap. It does not. Across all \num{18} units the refit moves balanced accuracy by at most \num{0.018} from the independent-support arm, and the two largest movements, both on PANDA, are downward; on \num{13} of the \num{18} units the movement is at most \num{0.002}. The gap to class-balanced cross-entropy meanwhile remains as large as \num{0.098}, on BRACS under the difficulty-reversed severe condition. The failure of the non-prevalence signals is a property of what those weights encode, not of how hard they were applied.

\section{Explaining Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:rq3}
Damage magnitude and recovery headroom vary across conditions, and RQ3 asks which realized shortages track that variation. Two association models are fitted, one for signed balanced-accuracy damage over the cross-entropy cells and one for recovery over the gate-passing method cells. Both use the same linear predictor over standardized quantities,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_j = \alpha + u_{g[j]} + \beta_{\rho}\log \rho_j + \beta_{\mathrm{ind}} S_{\mathrm{ind},j} + \beta_{A} A_j + \beta_{\mathrm{div}} S_{\mathrm{div},j},
  \label{eq:rq3-model}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\rho_j$ is achieved severity, $S_{\mathrm{ind},j}$ the independent-support shortage, $A_j$ the support--difficulty alignment, $S_{\mathrm{div},j}$ the diversity shortage, and $u_{g[j]}$ a random intercept over dataset--target groups that absorbs level differences between datasets. Observation errors come from the bootstrap distributions of the corresponding endpoints.

The damage model is fitted over the \num{18} cross-entropy cells and the recovery model over the gate-passing method cells with a defined recovery fraction; the matched-versus-unmatched contrast rows have no deficit denominator and therefore no recovery, so they enter neither. These models are exploratory by construction and by fitting procedure. They are estimated at the posterior mode and return point coefficients with no standard errors and no posterior intervals, so a coefficient reported here indicates direction and relative magnitude but licenses no claim of statistical significance. The only accuracy evidence available for them is the leave-one-group-out check, which refits each model with one dataset--target group held out and reports the held-out root mean squared error; a group whose held-out error is much larger than the rest indicates that the association does not transfer across datasets. Coefficients and that diagnostic appear in Tables~\ref{tab:rq3} and~\ref{tab:rq3-logo}.

\noindent Three readings follow from the damage model, each qualified by the absence of intervals. Achieved severity is the largest signal predictor, at $\beta_{\log\rho}=\num{0.0471}$ on standardized scale against a residual scale of $\sigma=\num{0.0184}$, which restates in model form the severity ordering already visible in Table~\ref{tab:discrimination-deficit}. Diversity shortage enters with a comparable magnitude and the opposite sign, $\beta_{\mathrm{div}}=-\num{0.0424}$; because severe conditions have both higher $\rho$ and higher measured diversity shortage, the two predictors are strongly collinear across only \num{18} units, and the negative coefficient is better read as the diversity term absorbing part of a severity effect the model has already assigned to $\log\rho$ than as evidence that losing representational diversity reduces damage. Support--difficulty alignment contributes almost nothing, $\beta_{A}=-\num{0.0008}$, which sits awkwardly beside the large assignment effects in Table~\ref{tab:discrimination-deficit}; the alignment predictor is a single correlation summarizing an entire condition, and on the two binary datasets it can take only the values $\pm 1$, so it is a coarse encoding of the assignment contrast rather than a null result about it. The independent-support coefficient is exactly zero, for the reason established in Section~\ref{sec:rq1-profiles}: its predictor has no variance.

The variance components are as informative as the slopes. The dataset--target random-intercept scale, $\sigma_u=\num{0.0484}$, is more than two and a half times the residual scale, so more of the variation in damage lies between datasets than the four signal predictors explain within them. The leave-one-group-out errors are consistent with that: holding out TCGA-UT gives a held-out error of \num{0.0131}, comparable to the residual scale, whereas holding out PANDA gives \num{0.0861}, larger than any deficit that dataset records except its own severe condition. PANDA contributes only two cells and has the largest severe deficit in the benchmark, so the model cannot place it from the other three groups. The association therefore does not transfer across datasets, and no coefficient here should be carried into a method design without a within-dataset check.

The recovery model, fitted over the \num{252} gate-passing method cells that have a defined recovery fraction, answers RQ3's second half in the negative. Its intercept of \num{0.5596} places average recovery a little above half the established deficit, but none of the four realized shortages accounts for the spread around that average: the three estimable slopes are $\beta_{\log\rho}=-\num{0.0115}$, $\beta_{A}=-\num{0.0242}$, and $\beta_{\mathrm{div}}=-\num{0.0290}$, all an order of magnitude smaller than the residual scale $\sigma=\num{0.4372}$. The variance components make the reason plain. The residual scale exceeds the dataset--target intercept scale $\sigma_u=\num{0.0404}$ by a factor of eleven, exactly reversing the ordering in the damage model, so almost all variation in recovery lies within a dataset rather than between datasets. What varies within a dataset in this model is the method, which the model deliberately does not include as a predictor. Recovery headroom on these conditions is therefore a property of which method was applied, not of which shortage the condition realized, which is the same conclusion Section~\ref{sec:rq2-family} reaches from the confirmatory family directly.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The preceding sections establish what the controlled allocation damaged, how much of that damage the tested methods returned, and which realized shortages tracked the variation. This section reads those outcomes as design evidence. The question it answers is not which existing method won, but what a mitigation method would have to read, and to do, in order to address the shortages these datasets actually present.

\subsection{What the evidence supports}
\label{sec:discussion-evidence}
The findings are summarized here by research question rather than by dataset, since the design intends datasets to be replicates of a mechanism rather than objects of interest in themselves. Three statements structure the summary, and every statement is bound to the gate status of the units it rests on, so that a claim about recovery is never carried by a unit in which no damage was established.

On RQ1, controlled allocation produced a material discrimination deficit in \num{12} of \num{18} comparison units and a material probability-quality deficit in six, all six of the latter on BRACS. Damage grows with achieved severity and with adversity of the tail assignment, and it is largest where the fixed budget is split between few classes, so that the tail retains almost none of it. The six units that opened neither gate all fail on magnitude rather than on precision: at $\rho=10$ on CAMELYON16 and TCGA-UT, and at $\rho=100$ on the difficulty-aligned TCGA-UT condition, redistributing the budget measurably but immaterially changes case-macro balanced accuracy. Damage did not fall where support rank alone predicts: under the difficulty-reversed assignment the body tier absorbs more loss than the tail.

On RQ2, recovery separated widely across the confirmatory family at a fixed intervention mechanism, but along a single split. Prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy recover most of both the discrimination and the probability-quality deficit wherever a gate opened; independent-support, difficulty, and diversity weighting recover essentially none of the discrimination deficit and actively degrade tail probability quality in \num{15} of \num{18} calibration cells. The matched-versus-unmatched contrast is positive only in the three units whose dominant shortage is nominal support, and negative in every unit whose dominant shortage is one of the other three, because in those units the matched arm holds exactly the member that does not work. The matched-$\beta$ diagnostic excludes tuned weight strength as the explanation.

On RQ3, achieved severity is the strongest signal-side predictor of damage magnitude, diversity shortage enters with a comparable magnitude but is collinear with severity across only \num{18} units, support--difficulty alignment contributes almost nothing under its single-correlation encoding, and independent-support shortage is fixed at zero by the design. Between-dataset variation exceeds within-dataset residual variation in the damage model, and the leave-one-group-out check shows the association does not transfer to PANDA. The recovery model reverses that ordering by a factor of eleven and gives all four shortages coefficients an order of magnitude below its residual scale, so no realized shortage explains recovery headroom; what varies is the method, which the model does not include. Both models carry point estimates only.

\subsection{Which shortage a mitigation method must read}
\label{sec:discussion-signal}
A method must estimate the shortage that binds, which makes the empirical question whether the dominant shortage was identifiable and whether it mattered. On these datasets the answer is that the dominant shortage was identifiable in \num{12} of \num{18} units and did not carry the recovery. Neither independent-support nor diversity shortage tracked damage that prevalence failed to track, and the two methods that read prevalence and nominal support recovered most of every deficit that was established. Under the outcome the protocol anticipated for this case, the simpler weighting is the correct default and the added shortage-estimation machinery buys nothing.

That conclusion has to be stated with the design boundary attached, because the boundary is what produced part of it. Independent-support shortage was not merely uninformative here; it was never created. The shared per-class evidence pool that makes the allocation contrast clean also holds independent support fixed across conditions, so the benchmark tests independent-support weighting on conditions in which the quantity it reads does not vary. The correct inference is that a prevalence-only method is sufficient for allocation-induced shortage under a fixed evidence pool, not that independent support is an unproductive signal in general. Testing that would require cohorts that differ in how many patients back a class, which this design deliberately excludes.

The frequency of the ambiguous designation qualifies the reading further. Six of \num{18} units, four of them at moderate severity, have their two largest shortage scores within a quarter of a standard deviation. A taxonomy that cannot separate the top two axes in a third of its conditions does not partition sharply enough to route a method, and in that case the design implication is a method that combines signals rather than one that selects among them.

\subsection{Signal against mechanism}
\label{sec:discussion-mechanism}
Because the five confirmatory methods share one intervention mechanism and differ only in the signal their weights read, the spread of recovery across them isolates how much leverage the choice of signal carries. That spread is wide, running from below zero to above \num{0.99} within single units, so signal choice does determine whether a weighted-loss method works at all. The spread is not, however, evidence that a better shortage estimate would help, because it is bimodal rather than graded: two members work almost everywhere and three work almost nowhere, with little in between. The productive direction that this pattern indicates is not a finer estimate of difficulty or diversity but an explanation of why weights derived from those signals fail to move the endpoint at all.

The comparison between prevalence-weighted and class-balanced cross-entropy on PANDA sharpens that point, since those two occupy adjacent corners of the taxonomy yet differ by a full deficit's worth of recovery, \num{0.968} against \num{0.118} at severe severity. What separates them is not the signal in the taxonomy's sense but the transform applied to it: the effective-number reweighting saturates at high imbalance on a binary target and returns nearly uniform weights. A signal taxonomy that treats these two as neighbours therefore under-describes the design space that actually determines recovery.

The matched-$\beta$ diagnostic separates these readings from a third possibility. A method may appear to win on its signal while in fact winning on a more aggressive tuned weight strength; refitting independent-support weighting at the class-balanced weight strength removes that confound. Here the refit changes balanced accuracy by at most \num{0.018} in any unit, and its largest movement, on PANDA at severe severity, is downward; the gap to class-balanced cross-entropy remains as large as \num{0.098}. The advantage that class-balanced weighting holds is therefore attributable to what its weights encode and not to how aggressively they are applied.

\subsection{Where recovery ceilings}
\label{sec:discussion-ceiling}
The units that define an unmet need would be those in which a gate opened and no method, confirmatory or exploratory, returned a substantial fraction of the deficit. There are none. In every gate-passing unit at least one tested method recovers more than \num{0.67} of the deficit, and the full roster reaches above \num{0.80} in nine of the \num{12} discrimination-gate units. The ceiling on these conditions therefore belongs to neither the data nor the frozen representation, and a new method cannot be motivated by damage that existing mechanisms leave unrepaired. It has to be motivated instead by what those mechanisms cost, by the endpoints they degrade while repairing another, or by the regimes in which they should not be applied.

The complementary case is equally informative for scoping. Six units opened no gate at all, and all six failed on magnitude rather than precision. A method that intervenes there spends capacity and risks degrading endpoints that were not damaged, and the calibration results in Section~\ref{sec:rq2-calibration} show that this risk is real rather than hypothetical: in cells where the wrong signal was applied, tail-group probability quality fell well below the untreated imbalanced reference. Knowing when not to act is part of the specification, not an afterthought.

\subsection{Design requirements and open questions}
\label{sec:discussion-requirements}
The preceding subsections resolve into a small set of requirements for a mitigation method, each traceable to a specific result and annotated with the strength of evidence behind it.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The method must read prevalence or nominal support, and must not rely on a difficulty or diversity estimate as its primary signal. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the five-family separation in Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory} on all \num{12} discrimination-gate units.
  \item It must specify the transform applied to that signal, not only the signal itself, because the two prevalence-corner members differ by a full deficit's worth of recovery on PANDA where the effective-number transform saturates. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the same table.
  \item The estimate must be formed per class and per condition rather than transferred across datasets, since the dataset--target intercept scale exceeds the residual scale and the leave-one-group-out error on PANDA is six times that on TCGA-UT. \emph{Exploratory}, from Tables~\ref{tab:rq3} and~\ref{tab:rq3-logo}.
  \item It must not degrade tail-group probability quality while repairing discrimination. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the \num{15} negative calibration-recovery cells in Table~\ref{tab:calibration-recovery}, \num{10} of which survive Holm adjustment.
  \item It must decline to intervene where allocation produced no material deficit, which on these datasets is every moderate condition on CAMELYON16 and TCGA-UT and the difficulty-aligned severe condition on TCGA-UT. \emph{Confirmatory}, from the gate routing in Table~\ref{tab:gate-routing}.
  \item It must not require access to test prevalence, labelled data beyond the training partition, or per-condition tuning that a deployment could not perform. \emph{Design constraint}, carried from the protocol rather than established by a result here.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The exploratory models of Section~\ref{sec:rq3} supply point estimates with no intervals and may therefore motivate a design direction but never justify one on their own.

Three questions remain outside this benchmark. It manipulates allocation within a fixed pool, so it says nothing about patient shortage as such. It also fixes Virchow2 features. Whether any gate-passing regime has unmet need is deferred to the exposure-matched rerun.

\section{Conclusion and Scope}
\label{sec:limits}
This report answers three questions in the order the protocol fixed: what controlled allocation damaged, how much of that damage signal-matched mitigation returned, and which realized shortages tracked the variation in both. Its contribution is the design evidence assembled in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, which states what a mitigation method must read, at what granularity, under what constraints, and where it should decline to act.

The legacy run cannot settle which signal recovers damage or whether a gate-passing condition resists repair. Those conclusions await the exposure-matched rerun.

Four scope conditions bound every claim above. The three partitions are locked design repetitions of one dataset, not independent cohorts, so the intervals quantify variation across partitionings and initializations rather than across populations. The association models of Section~\ref{sec:rq3} are exploratory and carry no causal interpretation, and their coefficients come without intervals. The benchmark covers patch classification only, where each prediction unit carries its own label; at whole-slide level, reallocating labelled slides changes nominal and independent support together, so the two signals could not be separated in the way this design requires. Temperature scaling is reported as a diagnostic throughout and never gates a decision.

\noindent Four deviations from the locked protocol were made, each before any definitive gate row was read, and each is recorded here with its reason. Every selection in Table~\ref{tab:tuning-selections} that terminated at the \num{1e-5} or \num{1e-2} envelope boundary is flagged there, since a boundary selection means the envelope rather than the validation data determined the choice.

The two deficit-gate thresholds were derived rather than assumed. The protocol had fixed $D_{\mathrm{BA}} \ge \num{0.02}$ and $D_{\mathrm{cal}} \ge \num{0.05}$ nats without a stated derivation, and the discrimination value conflicted with the support pilot's own materiality constant of \num{0.01} for the same endpoint under the same kind of manipulation. Both were replaced by quantities the protocol already fixes: the discrimination threshold became $\max(\num{0.01}, 2\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}})=\num{0.01443}$ and the calibration threshold $2\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}}=\num{0.18237}$ nats, where $\sigma_{\mathrm{seed}}$ is the largest across datasets and partitions of the confirmation-seed standard deviation of the corresponding balanced-condition endpoint. The change was applied identically to all four datasets and frozen before any gate row was read.

The gate-defining estimator was changed from patch-micro to case-macro, and the evidence cell from a per-partition cell to a cell pooled across a case's partition appearances. Both are corrections of a mismatch between a diagnostic and the estimator it certifies: the preflight weighted a case by its patch count, so one large annotated region or one macro-metastasis slide could carry more than half of a class's weight, and the per-partition cell certified an estimand that no reported quantity uses, since the bootstrap draws one shared weight per case over the union of the three test frames.

Cholangiocarcinoma was excluded from the TCGA-UT target set, reducing it from \num{31} eligible classes to \num{30}, because its held-out participant count could not support split-unit-clustered inference. The exclusion uses the existing construction-time mechanism and removes the class from training as well as evaluation, which is stronger than the evidence problem requires and was accepted in exchange for a uniform \num{30}-type task definition.

The legacy validation cadence was tied to update count. The rerun uses about 170 checkpoints per method-specific derived update count.

Three execution defects are recorded here rather than as protocol changes, because none of them altered an estimand, a threshold, or a selection rule.

The cross-dataset matching record, which standardizes the four shortage scores across all \num{18} units and assigns each its dominant shortage, is written per dataset root. It was produced for BRACS, CAMELYON16, and PANDA before their confirmatory aggregation ran, but for TCGA-UT it was produced afterwards, so the first aggregation of that dataset emitted no matched-versus-unmatched rows. The record was regenerated for TCGA-UT from the same four roots and the aggregation repeated; the dominant-shortage and ambiguity labels it assigns are identical to those the other three datasets used, and their standardized scores agree to within \num{0.005}. The other three datasets retain their original records, since re-deriving them would change no label. The repeated aggregation reproduced every TCGA-UT cross-entropy deficit exactly and added the eight matched-contrast rows the frozen matching record implies.

The recovery association model of Section~\ref{sec:rq3} initially returned no estimate. The matched-versus-unmatched contrast rows are contrasts between two arms rather than comparisons against the reference, so they carry no deficit denominator and no recovery fraction; including them among the model's observations propagated an undefined value through the whole fit. They are now excluded from the recovery cells, which is what the estimand in Equation~\eqref{eq:rq3-model} always implied. Thirteen of the \num{265} gate-passing method cells are excluded on this ground, and the damage model is unaffected.

The final aggregation pass for TCGA-UT exhausted its wall-clock allocation while writing the secondary interval tables, after the crossed comparison file on which every gate and recovery estimate in this report depends had already been written. Those interval tables are computed from the fitted runs and the partition databases alone, neither of which the repeated aggregation changed, so the tables carried over from the preceding complete pass are the same tables the interrupted pass would have written. Nothing reported here is drawn from a partially written artifact.
