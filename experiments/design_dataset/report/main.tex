\title{Class-Imbalance Benchmarks for Whole-Slide Images and Patches}
\author{Yannik Queisler}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Class imbalance in computational pathology appears both as a native property of clinical cohorts and as an experimental factor that can be controlled by resampling. This report studies both forms in patch-level and whole-slide-image (WSI) feature-bag benchmarks. TCGA-UT provides a pan-cancer setting in which training imbalance is varied artificially by power-law sampling while validation and test splits remain case-disjoint and fixed. BRACS provides an external breast-lesion setting in which the native distribution over seven histologic subtypes is measured first and used only if it is meaningfully imbalanced; otherwise the same power-law severity sweep is applied. Across both datasets, frozen Virchow2 features and a fixed evidence budget separate imbalance mechanisms from encoder training. The current TCGA-UT results show that method rankings are largely preserved across constructed severities while balanced accuracy declines monotonically as rare-class support diminishes; macro F1 is more stable between $\lambda=0.5$ and $\lambda=1.0$ before dropping at $\lambda=1.5$. The BRACS component is designed to test whether conclusions from the controlled pan-cancer setting transfer to a smaller, clinically annotated lesion-subtyping dataset.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A prior class-imbalance study on TCGA-UT compared mitigation methods under the dataset's native, moderately imbalanced slide distribution (head-to-tail ${\approx}28{:}1$) across 32 cancer types \citep{queisler2026imbalanceTCGA}. Native distributions are realistic but confound several factors simultaneously: how often each class appears, tumor morphology, tissue diversity, slide acquisition, and the representation geometry of the frozen encoder. A controlled construction isolates one factor---training-support frequency---by imposing known power-law distributions while holding validation and test data fixed. A native external dataset, in contrast, tests whether the same benchmark machinery remains meaningful when the class distribution is not designed by the experimenter.

TCGA-UT is a suitable test bed for this isolation because it provides slide labels and patch-level evidence from diagnostic whole-slide images in The Cancer Genome Atlas \citep{tomczak2015reviewThe,komura2022universalEncoding}. Controlling only the imbalance severity under a fixed native-prevalence class ordering allows the question to be posed cleanly: do mitigation methods maintain their relative advantage as the imbalance between frequent and rare cancer types intensifies along the same ranking?

BRACS adds a complementary native benchmark. It is an H\&E breast-pathology dataset with \num{547} WSIs and \num{4539} regions of interest (ROIs) from \num{189} patients, annotated by consensus of three board-certified pathologists into three lesion types and seven subtypes \citep{brancati2022bracs}. The atypical categories are clinically important and comparatively scarce, making BRACS a natural class-imbalance setting without imposing power-law resampling. Unlike TCGA-UT, BRACS is organ-specific, has fewer labels, and provides lesion-focused ROI annotations; these differences make it useful as an external check rather than a duplicate of the pan-cancer experiment.

The report therefore contains two benchmark roles. TCGA-UT evaluates the same method families as \citet{queisler2026imbalanceTCGA} under three constructed training distributions that vary only $\lambda \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.5\}$, the imbalance-severity parameter. BRACS evaluates the same patch and WSI-bag method families on its native seven-subtype distribution. Patch-level methods include cross-entropy, class weighting, balanced sampling, focal loss, Scholz-style metric losses, CFAL, OKO set learning, and ProGAN synthetic augmentation \citep{scholz2024imbalanceAware}. WSI-bag methods include cross-entropy, class weighting, balanced sampling, focal loss, RankMix, SC-MIL, and MDE-MIL \citep{chen2023rankmix,juyal2024scMil,ling2025mdeMil}. Patch and WSI-bag methods are reported separately because they consume different prediction units and answer regime-specific questions.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:dataset-construction}
The two datasets serve different roles. TCGA-UT is used for a controlled severity sweep in which the training distribution is designed by the experimenter. BRACS is first treated as a native external benchmark: its observed seven-subtype distribution is measured and used directly if it provides a meaningful class-imbalance setting. If the prepared BRACS distribution is too close to balanced to test imbalance mitigation, the same power-law construction used for TCGA-UT is applied to BRACS instead, with $\lambda \in \{0.5,1.0,1.5\}$.

\subsection{TCGA-UT Controlled Severity Benchmark}
The construction shares the TCGA-UT case-disjoint split protocol with \citet{queisler2026imbalanceTCGA}. For each of three random seeds, the validation and test splits are held fixed, and artificial imbalance is applied only to the training split. The assignment is patient-disjoint: all slides and feature rows associated with one TCGA participant remain in exactly one partition. This isolates the effect of training-support frequency: model selection and evaluation are comparable across imbalance parameters and mitigation methods without patient leakage. Strictly, the controlled lever is the per-class training-support \emph{distribution}: because each $\lambda$ selects a different number of slides per class from the shared pool, the specific slides realized for a class also change across $\lambda$. Differences observed across severities therefore reflect both the change in class counts and, secondarily, which slides happen to be sampled; the fixed validation and test splits and the shared slide pool limit but do not fully remove this slide-composition confound.

All 32 cancer types are retained. Let $C=32$, let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$ denote the seed-specific training slide pool, and let $a_c$ be the number of available training slides for class $c$. The constructed training split must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  1 \le n_c \le a_c,\qquad
  \sum_{c=1}^{C} n_c = N,\qquad
  c \in \{1,\ldots,C\}.
  \label{eq:full-scale-constraints}
\end{equation}
\noindent Every class receives at least one slide, no class is oversampled beyond its available pool, and the total training set is fixed at $N$ slides shared across all imbalance parameters and seeds.

After slide selection, the input evidence is capped. Patch classifiers use up to 30 deterministic patches per slide. WSI-bag classifiers use up to 30 deterministic frozen feature instances per slide. In both regimes, Virchow2 features remain frozen \citep{vorontsov2024virchow,zimmermann2024virchow2}, represented as the concatenation of the CLS token and the mean of the patch tokens ($\num{2560}$-dimensional vectors). The cap aligns the input budget across regimes and prevents slides with more extracted tumor patches from dominating the training objective.

\subsection{Power-Law Training Support}
For an ordered class list $(c_1,\ldots,c_C)$, the intended share for rank $i$ under imbalance parameter $\lambda$ is
\begin{equation}
  p_i(\lambda) =
  \frac{i^{-\lambda}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} j^{-\lambda}}.
  \label{eq:power-law}
\end{equation}
\noindent When $\lambda=0$ the intended distribution is uniform over ranks; as $\lambda$ increases, early-ranked classes receive more training support and late-ranked classes receive less. All constructed splits use the \emph{native-prevalence order}: classes are ranked by their TCGA-UT training-split slide count, so rank 1 is the most frequent cancer type and rank $C=32$ the least frequent. The power-law form is imposed to obtain controlled, monotonic severity steps; it is not a fit to the native TCGA-UT distribution, which is only moderately imbalanced (${\approx}28{:}1$) and not well described by a single power law.

Raw targets $N\,p_i(\lambda)$ are converted to integer counts by flooring and assigning residual slides by largest fractional remainder. Each class first receives one guaranteed slide; the remaining $N-C$ slides are then allocated according to Equation~\eqref{eq:power-law}. A regime is accepted only when every rounded target satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq:full-scale-constraints}.

\subsection{Feasible Pool Size and Realized Profiles}
\label{sec:class-orders}
The pool size $N$ must be small enough that no class target exceeds its available support across all evaluated imbalance parameters. Define the maximum feasible pool for a given $\lambda$ and seed as
\begin{equation}
  T^*(\lambda) = \min_{c} \frac{a_c}{p_c(\lambda)}.
  \label{eq:tstar}
\end{equation}
\noindent Equation~\eqref{eq:tstar} is a continuous bound on the real-valued targets $N\,p_c(\lambda)$ and does not by itself account for the guaranteed-one floor, flooring, and largest-remainder steps of the integer allocation. The operational pool size is therefore the largest total whose \emph{integer} allocation satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq:full-scale-constraints} on every seed, found by a feasibility search that runs the full rounding rule and rejects any total that overruns a class capacity. The continuous bound $\lfloor\min_{s} T^*_s(0.5)\rfloor$ and this integer-verified value coincide for the TCGA-UT splits, so reporting the closed-form bound is exact here; the integer check is what guarantees feasibility in general. The binding regime is $\lambda=0.5$ rather than the steepest: the flat power-law profile at $\lambda=0.5$ requires every class---including rare ones with limited available slides---to contribute a non-trivial share, saturating the rarest class's supply at a lower total $N$ than the peaked profiles at $\lambda\ge 1.0$. For the TCGA-UT training splits, this yields $N = \num{1132}$ slides, approximately 18\% of the full available training pool.

\subsection{BRACS Native Benchmark}
BRACS is prepared as a second benchmark because it changes the data regime without changing the evaluation question. It is an organ-specific H\&E breast-lesion dataset rather than a pan-cancer dataset, and its annotations are lesion-subtype labels rather than cancer-type labels. The dataset contains \num{547} WSIs and \num{4539} ROIs from \num{189} patients; WSIs and ROIs were annotated by consensus of three board-certified pathologists \citep{brancati2022bracs}. The labels form three lesion types---benign, atypical, and malignant---and seven subtypes: normal tissue (N), pathological benign (PB), usual ductal hyperplasia (UDH), flat epithelial atypia (FEA), atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and invasive carcinoma (IC).

The benchmark uses the seven-subtype target because it is the level at which class imbalance is most relevant. The atypical categories FEA and ADH are clinically important precancerous lesions and are less common than many benign or malignant samples, so collapsing BRACS to the three lesion types would hide part of the rare-class problem. Patient identifiers from the BRACS summary sheet define the split unit. The prepared benchmark therefore enforces patient-disjoint train, validation, and test partitions across three seeds, matching the leakage-control standard used for TCGA-UT.

BRACS evidence is derived from the ROI set. Each annotated ROI is tiled deterministically into $256\times256$ RGB patches, capped at 30 tiles per ROI to match the evidence budget used for TCGA-UT. Patch methods consume one frozen Virchow2 feature per ROI tile. The WSI-bag pipeline consumes grouped ROI-tile features as bounded bags, preserving the same patch-versus-bag comparison while avoiding an additional tissue-detection step on full WSIs. This choice prioritizes label fidelity: all training instances originate from pathologist-selected lesion regions rather than from unlabeled background tissue.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
The method taxonomy follows the companion benchmark, but this report uses it to study controlled training distributions rather than only the native TCGA-UT distribution. Cross-entropy is the no-mitigation reference. Data-level methods change minibatch exposure through balanced sampling or synthetic augmentation. Algorithm-level methods change the per-example loss through class weighting, focal loss, metric-derived objectives, prototype-affinity objectives, or OKO set supervision. WSI-specific methods change how slide bags are represented or mixed.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation}
Each TCGA-UT constructed split is indexed by seed and imbalance parameter $\lambda \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.5\}$. Hyperparameters are selected independently for every method and every $\lambda$: for each candidate configuration the validation macro F1 is averaged over the three seeds, and the configuration with the highest mean is chosen, with ties broken by mean validation balanced accuracy. A single configuration is therefore fixed per method and $\lambda$ (shared across seeds) and is not reused across $\lambda$. Because tuning is performed separately at each severity, the reported figures reflect each method at its per-$\lambda$ best, which can partly absorb the effect of imbalance severity; the comparison thus targets achievable rather than fixed-configuration performance. Selected configurations are evaluated on the fixed test split, and results are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across seeds. Macro F1 is the primary discrimination metric because it weights classes equally. Balanced accuracy and accuracy are reported as complementary discrimination metrics.

BRACS follows the same validation-selection rule, but the regime index depends on the prepared distribution. If the native seven-subtype support is meaningfully imbalanced, one native configuration is selected per method by averaging validation macro F1 across the three patient-disjoint seeds. If the native support is not a useful class-imbalance setting, BRACS is switched to the same $\lambda \in \{0.5,1.0,1.5\}$ power-law construction as TCGA-UT, and tuning is performed per $\lambda$.

The comparison is intentionally regime-specific. Patch-feature methods are compared only with other patch-feature methods, and WSI-bag methods are compared only with other WSI-bag methods. The two regimes share the case-disjoint splits, frozen Virchow2 representation family, and 30-instance evidence budget, but they differ in prediction unit and aggregation mechanism.

Calibration is evaluated alongside discrimination using negative log likelihood (NLL), multiclass Brier score \citep{brier1950verification}, and expected calibration error \citep[ECE;][]{naeini2015obtaining,guo2017calibration}. For each method and seed, a single temperature is fit by minimizing NLL on the validation split and applied unchanged to the test score matrix before softmax \citep{guo2017calibration}. Tables~\ref{tab:calibration-patch} and~\ref{tab:calibration-wsi} report ECE before and after temperature scaling; full NLL and Brier metrics are given in Tables~\ref{tab:calibration-patch-full} and~\ref{tab:calibration-wsi-full}.

Patch-feature balanced accuracy shows a consistent, monotone decline as imbalance intensifies, whereas macro F1 is comparatively flat between $\lambda=0.5$ and $\lambda=1.0$---rising slightly for several methods (CFAL, CE+soft-F1, OKO, ProGAN)---before dropping at $\lambda=1.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:patch-results}). Focal loss is typically among the weakest of the remaining methods, though by margins within seed-to-seed variation. The support--recall relationship makes the locus of the severity effect explicit (Figure~\ref{fig:support-recall}): per-class recall is near-saturated ($\approx\num{0.9}$) for high-support classes and degrades sharply for low-support classes, with the $\lambda=1.5$ points dominating the low-support, low-recall corner. The tier-resolved tables confirm that the aggregate decline is concentrated in the rare classes. Head-tier macro F1 is essentially flat across severity---patch head F1 holds near \num{0.79} and only edges down to ${\approx}\num{0.77}$ at $\lambda=1.5$, and WSI-bag head F1 stays near \num{0.87}---whereas tail-tier F1 falls substantially: from ${\approx}\num{0.53}$ to ${\approx}\num{0.46}$ in the patch regime and from ${\approx}\num{0.69}$ to ${\approx}\num{0.61}$ in the WSI-bag regime. The head--tail gap therefore widens with $\lambda$, and mitigation methods differ almost entirely in how well they protect the tail rather than the already-saturated head. In the WSI-bag regime at $\lambda=1.5$, the bag-composition methods retain the highest tail F1 (SC-MIL \num{0.643}, RankMix \num{0.636}), MDE-MIL's tail erodes to \num{0.610} despite its strong head and body tiers---mirroring its aggregate erosion at high severity---and focal loss is weakest in the tail (\num{0.555}). These tail-tier differences are small relative to their seed-to-seed standard deviations (up to ${\approx}\num{0.07}$), so they should be read as numerical rather than statistically separated.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The TCGA-UT controlled construction reveals two complementary patterns. First, method rankings are broadly preserved across imbalance severities, with moderate reshuffling among closely performing methods at the extremes. Second, balanced accuracy declines monotonically with $\lambda$ for almost all methods, with a total range of eight to nine percentage points from the mildest to the strongest constructed regime. These patterns suggest that the relative utility of mitigation methods is largely robust to the particular severity chosen within this range, while absolute performance degrades substantially as rare-class support diminishes.

At the patch level, declines span from approximately 7.8 percentage points for OKO to 10.5 for ProGAN augmentation. ProGAN leads all patch methods at the mildest severity ($\lambda=0.5$, BAcc \num{0.729}) but drops to last at the harshest ($\lambda=1.5$, BAcc \num{0.624})---a reversal that reflects the difficulty of training patch-level generative models when tail-class support falls to single digits: synthetic features complement real training data at mild imbalance but cannot compensate for critically scarce patches at severe imbalance. OKO and CE+soft-F1 maintain their relative advantage at the harshest severity; focal loss is typically among the weakest of the remaining methods, by margins within seed-to-seed variation.

In the WSI-bag regime, RankMix stands out for its stability, declining by only 6.5 percentage points from $\lambda=0.5$ to $\lambda=1.5$. Focal MIL is the least stable, dropping by nearly 12 percentage points and landing at the bottom of the WSI rankings at $\lambda=1.5$. MDE-MIL declines more than RankMix and falls narrowly below Weighted CE at the harshest regime, showing that consistency regularisation provides diminishing returns as rare-class support becomes critically scarce. RankMix is consistently among the strongest WSI-bag methods and the most robust at $\lambda=1.5$; SC-MIL stays competitive but does not uniformly beat loss-based baselines---at $\lambda=0.5$ it trails Weighted CE and focal loss---so the advantage of WSI-specific bag-composition is clear for RankMix but only partial for SC-MIL.

Calibration analysis complements discrimination: CFAL leads on patch discrimination but requires the strongest temperature correction and retains the highest post-scaling ECE. In the WSI-bag regime, RankMix is already well calibrated before scaling, while MDE-MIL shows only marginal improvement from temperature scaling despite leading on discrimination at mild and moderate severities.

The BRACS extension changes the status of the evidence. TCGA-UT answers a controlled question: what happens as a known frequency profile is imposed on one pan-cancer dataset? BRACS asks whether the same patch and WSI-bag methodology is meaningful on a clinically annotated breast-lesion dataset whose native subtype distribution may or may not be sufficiently imbalanced. This distinction is important. If BRACS is naturally imbalanced after patient-disjoint preparation, it provides an external validity check under the dataset's observed clinical distribution. If it is not, evaluating imbalance methods on the native split would be weak evidence, so the benchmark switches to the same power-law construction used for TCGA-UT.

Taken together, the current TCGA-UT results show that when training-support frequency is imposed directly via a power-law distribution, aggregate discrimination declines predictably but modestly with severity, and the method ranking remains informative across the range tested. The prediction unit---patch versus WSI-bag---continues to be the dominant determinant of absolute performance: WSI-bag methods achieve balanced accuracy approximately ten percentage points higher than their patch-feature counterparts at equivalent imbalance. BRACS is intended to test whether that regime-specific conclusion persists in an organ-specific lesion-subtyping dataset.

\section{Conclusion}
This report defines class-imbalance benchmarks for both patch-level and WSI-bag computational pathology. The TCGA-UT component keeps all 32 cancer types, preserves patient-disjoint validation and test splits, and varies only the training-support frequency under native-prevalence class ordering at $\lambda \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.5\}$. The shared pool of \num{1132} slides is determined by the mildest parameter $\lambda=0.5$, whose flat profile saturates the rarest class's available supply earliest. The three regimes span head-to-tail ratios of approximately 6:1, 30:1, and 122:1, bracketing and extending the native TCGA-UT ratio (${\approx}28{:}1$).

The TCGA-UT results show that method rankings are broadly preserved across all three constructed severities, while balanced accuracy declines by eight to eleven percentage points from the mildest to the strongest regime. At the patch level, ProGAN augmentation leads at mild severity ($\lambda=0.5$, BAcc \num{0.729}) and undergoes the steepest decline (10.5 pp), reversing from best to worst as tail-class support diminishes; OKO and CE+soft-F1 are most robust at the harshest severity. At the WSI-bag level, RankMix is consistently among the strongest methods and is the most severity-robust; SC-MIL remains competitive but does not uniformly outperform loss-based baselines, and MDE-MIL's advantage over those baselines erodes under the most extreme imbalance. The prediction unit remains the dominant performance determinant: WSI-bag methods achieve balanced accuracy roughly 12 percentage points above patch-feature methods at equivalent imbalance.

The BRACS component extends the benchmark design to seven-class breast-lesion subtyping with patient-disjoint splits and ROI-derived patch and bag evidence. Its native subtype distribution is used only if preparation confirms that it is a meaningful class-imbalance setting; otherwise BRACS is evaluated under the same three power-law severity levels as TCGA-UT. This conditional design avoids benchmarking imbalance interventions on a dataset whose prepared target distribution is effectively balanced.
